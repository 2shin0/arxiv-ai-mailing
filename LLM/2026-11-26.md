## 1. Knowledge-Base Gradient Descent: Self-Structuring Learning through Question Answering
- **Authors**: Masahiro Kato
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34841)
- **Abstract**:
> We consider the problem of constructing knowledge bases for applications based on large language models, in which the structure of a knowledge base determines how an application can retrieve and connect the information needed to answer a question. We study Self-Structuring Learning, a setting in which questions and expected answers guide the construction of a persistent knowledge base whose editable structure can be a graph schema or the organization of linked knowledge documents. We propose Knowledge-Base Gradient Descent, which diagnoses discrepancies between expected answers and responses obtained through the current knowledge base. At each round, the method proposes a structural edit, rebuilds the knowledge base, and adopts the edit when the learning loss decreases. The retained structure is evaluated on other questions about the same sources. By randomizing over candidate edits, we express the loss change of an edit as a derivative with respect to the probability of selecting it, and we evaluate joint edits as single candidates because their changes interact. Schema Descent instantiates the method for graph schemas. In experiments on large documents, each a bundle of research papers, the updated knowledge cards and graph schemas obtain higher mean Answer F1 on held-out generated questions than their initial structures, with schema gains in every document. On human questions, mean F1 rises for schemas and falls for cards: the updated structures serve the kind of question they were learned from.

## 2. TASK Memory: Beyond Text-Only Memory with Activation Steering and Knowledge Editing against Memory Hallucination
- **Authors**: Jinkun Zhao , Wenjun Wu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34762)
- **Abstract**:
> LLM agents increasingly rely on external memory, yet they still hallucinate about what they remember. Often the correct memory is retrieved and placed in the prompt, but the answer contradicts it or the memory is applied to the wrong question. We call these use-time memory hallucinations. Across five open models, we find that they arise from the conflict between parametric knowledge and external memory and take three forms: memory-ignoring, where the model returns to a stale fact in its weights (up to 46% of queries); memory-misapplying, where it attributes the memory to unrelated entities (up to 24%, and 49% when retrieval is combined with weight editing); and version confusion, where 53% of answers about revised facts assert the superseded value when both versions remain stored. We propose TASK memory, which goes beyond text-only memory by combining a Text note with Activation Steering and Knowledge editing that share one in-model address. The text holds the content. A removable knowledge edit supplies the address through its subject key and supports multi-hop composition. A pair of steering vectors decides, query by query, whether the model adopts the memory or keeps its own answer; the vectors store no content and control how the text memory is used. On frozen models under a pre-registered, test-once protocol, TASK reduces memory-ignoring hallucinations to at most 1.5% in all ten model-dataset cells and cuts neighbour misuse from 39.2% to 7.5% (+10.8 pp composite on CounterFact). It improves a 300-memory library over BM25 RAG by 16.4 pp, an advantage that holds at 3,000 memories, resolves fact revisions without fact IDs, and adds 7.5 pp of multi-hop accuracy on MQuAKE. 21 of 23 cross-model comparisons (8B-27B, four families) are significant, and TASK beats three recent context-steering methods in 9 of 10 model-dataset cells.

## 3. What Is a Theory Worth to an LLM Solver? Measuring, Inducing, and Merging Theories in Context
- **Authors**: Jun Gao , Shuchao Bi
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34751)
- **Abstract**:
> Mathematicians solve problems faster when they know the right theory. We ask how much a stated theory is worth to a large language model (LLM) solver, and whether a model can write that theory itself. In a length-matched protocol, adding the right theory to the context of Qwen3-14B raises pass@1 on pattern waiting-time problems from 0.37 to 0.83 and cuts the median tokens of correct solutions from 10.2k to 6.3k, while worked examples of the same length do not help. The gain shrinks as the solver and its token budget grow (+0.46, +0.26, +0.07), and at a long budget it comes almost entirely from finishing within budget: solutions that finish are correct 98--100\% of the time in every condition. To separate induction from recall, we plant unpublished theorems in custom probability families and measure how much of the oracle theory's gain an induced theory recovers. A strong open model, required to explore the training problems with code before writing, recovers 60\% of the gain on one planted family, 48\% on a second, and almost none on a third, where it finds the right form but not the condition that makes the form computable. No single induced note is complete, but a strong model that merges several notes recovers 61\% of the gain on the classic family, above the best single note (47\%). Theories are worth the reasoning they save, and models can find their pieces before they can assemble them.

## 4. ZeroHero: Training Small Language Agents from Scratch
- **Authors**: Nikita Sorokin , Zoya Volovikova , Alexey Skrynnik , Aleksandr Panov
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34635](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34635)
- **Abstract**:
> Large pretrained language models provide capable policies for interactive tasks, but how much model capacity is necessary for a specialized language agent? We introduce ZeroHero, a framework for training small language agents (SLAs) from scratch. In each environment, a large language model acts as the expert, and we collect its successful trajectories. We pretrain each SLA, which has fewer than 50 million parameters, to predict the expert's responses given the interaction history. We then post-train the agent on its own interactions using proximal policy optimization (PPO). During post-training, reasoning alignment supplements environment rewards with expert supervision on the language the agent generates alongside its actions. We evaluate the agents in three multistep environments where acting requires both understanding and generating language. In Crafter, agents interpret textual observations and generate reasoning before acting. In WebShop, they translate shopping instructions into search queries. In Alfworld-Dialogue, they ask clarifying questions and use the answers to guide household actions. The agents approach expert performance on WebShop and surpass their respective demonstration experts on Crafter training rollouts and held-out Alfworld-Dialogue tasks. ## 5. Do We Really Need an LLM for Weight-Space Programming? A Deterministic Forward-Only Paradigm and Its Capability Boundaries
- **Authors**: Lin Yao
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34612](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34612)
- **Abstract**:
> Recent work on Weight-Space Programming uses LLMs to edit neural weights with forward passes only, conflating LLM scripting with the underlying algebra of forward-only editing. We show that for core structured editing regimes, an LLM optimizer is unnecessary, and introduce Forward Weight-Space Programming (FWSP): a deterministic pipeline combining probing, localization, stratified operators (O1-O4), automatic blind routing (83.3% vs. 33.3% for a 27B LLM with the same tool library), and paired verification with zero additional per-token computation. For reasoning LLMs, we propose Protected FisherGate, a closed-form covariance-whitened readout edit orthogonal to normal-reasoning states: on DeepSeek-R1-0528-8B, a single frozen math-calibrated patch achieves 84.2% HumanEval pass@1 (1K budget; 75.4 +/- 9.4% across 3/5 Stage-4-passing subsets), beating 512-token-reserved budget forcing by +14.6 pp (-23.4% tokens) while transferring across HF-to-vLLM backends on full GSM8K and MATH-500. Across five modalities and thirteen models (50K to 27B dense and FP8 MoE), we further prove and verify two operator boundaries, subspace entanglement and frozen representation span, where FWSP-localized LoRA bridges forward-only and gradient adaptation.

## 6. vSFT: Learning to Verbally Tune Agents at Test Time
- **Authors**: Qishuai Wen , Rui Chen , Haiwen Li , Zhiyuan Huang , Qiwen Gu , Bingjie Gao , Geng Li , Jing Tang , Xiangxiang Chu , Chun-Guang Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34440)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model agents are increasingly improved via agentic reinforcement learning, which can be on-policy but suffers from expensive rollouts and sparse rewards, limiting its application at test time. In this paper, we present a verbally Supervised Fine-Tuning (vSFT) framework that enables the conjunction of dense feedback in natural language and weight updates at test time. Specifically, our vSFT forms a closed loop between an agent and a supervisor via a hypernetwork, in which the supervisor generates verbal supervision from the agent's failed trajectories, and the hypernetwork converts that supervision into low-rank weight updates to tune the agent. We validate that this test-time in-parameter evolving process outperforms both its in-context counterparts and prevalent reinforcement learning methods. In particular, our vSFT using Qwen3-4B outperforms GRPO and Reflexion by 8.7% and 13.9% in success rate on ALFWorld, and by 4.8% and 18.2% on WebShop, respectively. We believe that learning how to adapt a model, rather than an adapted model, offers a promising path towards more capable agents that improve themselves.

## 7. EvoLink-Bench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Self-Evolving Agents in Long-Horizon Interdependent Task Streams
- **Authors**: Wei Chen , Xiaodong Lu , Weihao Shen , Gongxun Li , Xingyu Guo , Qihang Sun , Fuwei Zhang , Zhao Zhang , Yikun Ban , Shuang Li , Deqing Wang , Fuzhen Zhuang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34400)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) agents are evolving from passive chatbots into autonomous systems that operate continuously in dynamic environments. Such agents need not only solve current tasks but also learn from past interactions to improve future performance. However, existing streaming benchmarks often rely on short task sequences or largely independent tasks, providing limited evidence of whether agents can effectively reuse experience when later tasks depend on earlier outcomes. To address this gap, we introduce \textbf{EvoLink-Bench}, a benchmark for evaluating self-evolving agents in long-horizon, interdependent task streams. It covers daily office work, multi-turn dialogue, and AutoResearch-style workflows, with persistent workspaces and dependencies across tasks. The benchmark includes two complementary levels: \textbf{L1}, which evaluates the acquisition and revision of language-expressible knowledge, and \textbf{L2}, which assesses the development of task-level capabilities through execution and feedback. Its evaluation further distinguishes within-task recovery, persistent-state updates, and experience reuse in later tasks. We evaluate five agent frameworks across three backbone configurations under three persistence conditions, covering 19,635 scored task slots from selected evaluation cohorts. Results show that no framework consistently performs best across both levels, retries recover only a limited proportion of failed tasks, and persistent-state updates do not necessarily improve subsequent performance. Furthermore, the effects of evolution differ between knowledge- and capability-level tasks, suggesting that these two forms of evolution require different learning mechanisms. Performance gains also vary across domains and task streams, with improvements on some tasks not necessarily extending to others.

## 8. MeterRouter: Budget-Aware Dynamic Routing for Multi-Agent Systems
- **Authors**: Yue Xu , Leikun Yin , Ruiming Min , Yufeng Lai , Justin Andrew Johnson , Sitao Luan , Xiaotong Shen
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34327)
- **Abstract**:
> Multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large language models (LLMs) outperform single-agent systems, but inter-agent communication incurs substantial token overhead. While assigning a large LLM to every agent is common, it results in inefficient resource utilization, leaving the system far from the Pareto frontier. The cost of answering a query is set jointly by how many agents are recruited, the role and LLM assigned to each, and communication topology among them. Most existing routers treat cost only implicitly rather than as an explicit optimization constraint. A fixed accuracy-cost preference also cannot express how much a caller is willing to pay for a particular query. We introduce MeterRouter, a budget-aware dynamic router that incrementally synthesizes a collaboration graph by selecting each agent's role, LLM, incoming connections, and termination conditioned on the query and a per-query monetary budget. We initialize the router by imitating successful execution graphs, then jointly refine its decisions with a variant of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) whose reward favors correct answers and penalizes spending and budget overruns. Across five benchmarks and a three-tier LLM pool, MeterRouter is (1) accurate, achieving performance comparable to the strongest MAS; (2) economical, reaching that accuracy at 82% lower cost than the strongest MAS router and extending the accuracy-cost frontier beyond the ceiling of single-agent systems; and (3) controllable, adapting its spending to the specified budget while rarely exceeding it.

## 9. Crucible: Evaluating LLM Agents in Executable Chemistry Research Environments
- **Authors**: Yinjun Liu , Xinye Li , Ruisen Peng , Lang Gao , Zixiang Xu , Zirui Song , Xiuying Chen
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34254)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) perform well on chemistry knowledge benchmarks, which mainly assess question answering and isolated tasks. Chemical research, however, requires using specialized tools and interpreting their outputs to obtain chemically valid results. To address this gap, we introduce Crucible Bench to evaluate this ability across all four stages of the design-make-test-analyze (DMTA) cycle. The benchmark contains 1,078 tasks in 12 task families curated from 18 published datasets, covering molecular design, synthesis planning, experimental execution, closed-loop optimization, simulation, and analytical characterization. Agents access 75 domain-specific tools through the Model Context Protocol in seven isolated environments. Sandboxed graders verify the resulting synthesis routes, experimental protocols, and other outputs. Laboratory tasks use robotic simulators or previously measured experimental data. We evaluate 14 LLMs as agents and find that the best achieves an overall score of 0.545, with synthesis planning and closed-loop optimization emerging as the hardest domains. Overall performance does not predict optimization performance well: the model ranked twelfth overall achieves the highest score in closed-loop optimization. Error analysis also shows that agents often submit chemically invalid results despite running their tools successfully. Stronger models benefit more from tool access, but reliable use of chemical software remains a challenge that Crucible Bench can track as models improve.

## 10. MedSkill-E$^{2}$: Evaluating and Evolving Skills for Medical Agents
- **Authors**: Jingjing Hua , Shaocong Wei , Jianghan Shen , Junzhi Ning , Min Cen , Cheng Tang , Ziyan Huang , Jiepeng Li , Fengshuo Liu , Kangkun Mao , Mouxiao Bian , Yirong Chen , Ming Hu , Jiyao Liu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34250)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use skills to access domain knowledge and complete real-world tasks. We observe that even when a skill specifies the rules and procedures needed for a task, agents may fail to apply them effectively during autonomous execution, a phenomenon we call skill Enable But Underuse (EBU). This issue is particularly consequential in medical tasks, where overlooking a single critical constraint can yield a plausible but incorrect result. To evaluate and address EBU, we introduce MedSkill-E$^{2}$, a framework for evaluating and evolving skills for medical agents. We construct EBU-Bench, a challenging benchmark covering 12 medically relevant task groups and associated skills, to evaluate agents' ability to use available skills autonomously. We quantify the EBU gap as the difference in skill-use accuracy between autonomous use and explicitly guided reading and application of the same skill. On EBU-Bench, all evaluated model-harness combinations exhibit EBU, with a mean gap of 43.94 percentage points (pp). For the same model, skill-use accuracy under autonomous use differs by up to 38.96 pp across harnesses. Motivated by these observations, we propose EBU-Evolve, which uses execution feedback from autonomous and explicitly guided skill use to distill effective guidance into reusable skill instructions. Relative to their pre-evolution versions, the revised skills improve skill-use accuracy during autonomous execution by 33.33 to 55.17 pp and continue to yield improvements when transferred across multiple models and harnesses. Our results show that autonomous skill-use accuracy varies across harnesses with the model and skill held fixed, and model rankings can change. EBU can persist even after a skill is opened. EBU-Evolve improves autonomous skill-use accuracy on unseen tasks within the same skill, with gains transferring across models and harnesses.

- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34247)
- **Abstract**:

## 13. PROVE: Mitigating Long-Horizon LLM Collusion with Reinforcement Learning from Verified Evidence
- **Authors**: Haochen Luo , Jie Fu , Mukai Li , Yuqi Wang , Qi Liu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34215](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34215)
- **Abstract**:
> When LLM agents verify each other's work over a long interaction, they drift into collusion: they come to accept work without the evidence that the verification protocol requires. Collusion is absent at the start and builds up episode by episode, which we trace to accurate feedback accumulating in memory. An agent that follows the rule is told that its verdict was wrong whenever its peer was right, so breaking the rule is the reward-maximizing choice, and agents talk themselves into it in their private reflections. Existing mitigations leave the agents unchanged; we train the behavior out. PROVE (Protocol Reinforcement from Verified Evidence) is a multi-agent reinforcement learning method that rewards verdicts by the evidence behind them rather than by their outcome, and credits every call an agent makes, including the calls that solve its own task, with the outcome that call controls. On Qwen3.6-27B, a LoRA adapter trained on six-episode interactions takes the collusion rate on held-out task sequences at the original ten-episode horizon from 65.0% to 1.0%, with no detectable change in task success. Collusion that converged in every interaction now converges in none, and the gain holds four episodes beyond the longest interaction seen in training. The trained 27B model colludes less than every model evaluated in the original study, closed-weight frontier models included. Code is available at this https URL .

## 14. SOLO: Towards Sample-Faithful and Detectable LLM Watermarking under Repeated Sampling via Content-Anchored Single-Owner Contexts
- **Authors**: Zhongliang Guo , Yan Lin , Yifei Qian , Shuai Zhao , Luwei Xiao , Daizong Liu , Chun Pong Lau
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34190](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34190)
- **Abstract**:
> Providers watermark the outputs of large language models, while applications sample many responses per prompt for self-consistency, best-of-n selection, and uncertainty estimation. We show that deployed configurations of distortion-free watermarks, which deduplicate keyed contexts only within a response, couple the responses of a request: answer templates make all responses query the watermark with the same context before the answer, so collisions across responses, usually assumed rare, become the norm. Key pools reduce or avoid the coupling at the cost of multiple testing, and the exact remedy of a context history shared across the request leaves many positions unwatermarked. On five language models from three families and three benchmarks, SOLO preserves self-consistency and uncertainty estimation and, at a 1% false positive rate, detects more responses than four-token key pools on most tasks, also after randomly editing 10% of the tokens, and more than the context history on most clean tasks. ## 15. Budget, Readout or Reasoning? Where Small Reasoning Models Lose Value in Auction Bidding
- **Authors**: Mahbod Khaleti , Seyed Reza Tavakoli , Baran Hoseini , Alireza Noori , Amir Amini , Koorosh Aslansefat , Marjan Alirezaie , Ali Habibnia
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34068)
- **Abstract**:
> Ad platforms and marketplaces show bidders a bid landscape, the estimated chance of winning at each candidate bid, and increasingly let agents built on reasoning language models choose the bid. We give six open reasoning models of 4B to 14B parameters such landscapes, with win curves from eBay closing prices and from a simulated advertising market (AuctionNet), and score their bids on held-out auctions against an exact calculator. Because the agent is handed the estimates, the lost value can be split by cause. Two failures belong to the models. They pass over the best option even when the values written in their own trace already rank it first, and their wrong answers lean toward the highest bid on the menu. The lean is about the bid's size, not its place in the list, and it survives sampling. Two further losses come from operator choices. A token cap cuts traces short: allowed to stop on their own within 4,096 tokens, five models recover 91-100% of the calculator's lead over a model that does no reasoning. Asking for the answer while the reasoning block is still open misreads it: closing the block first lifts the weakest model from 42% to 84% on eBay and from 24% to 81% on AuctionNet. Finally, an audit that flags bids disagreeing with the trace's own numbers, and overrides them with the calculator's bid, raises the share of the calculator's lead recovered at 768 forced tokens from 42-85% to 84-100% on eBay under nested cross-validation, and the same reader settings transfer from eBay to AuctionNet.

## 16. Few-Shot Adaptation of Vision-Language Models via Agentic Model Editing
- **Authors**: Yusheng Zhao , Xiao Luo , Zhiping Xiao , Ming Zhang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34036)
- **Abstract**:
> Few-shot adaptation transfers pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to new domains using a small set of labeled images. Most methods fit prompts, representations, or classifiers within an adaptation procedure specified in advance. Yet the visual distinctions needed for recognition can differ substantially across domains, motivating the question: Can an LLM agent use few-shot examples and model observations to design the adaptation procedure itself? We propose Agentic Model Editing (AME), an agentic framework that jointly designs input transformations, encoder edits, feature computation, and classifier fitting as an executable, domain-customized adaptation program. The agent inspects images, class semantics, model parameters, intermediate representations, and activations to formulate hypotheses about the target task. Across eleven datasets with CLIP ViT-B/16, AME achieves the highest average accuracies of 81.2% without back-propagation and 81.9% with back-propagation among the compared methods. Further experiments demonstrate applicability across VLM backbones and LLM agents.

## 17. PatternMiner: Automated Mathematical Discovery Through LLM-Guided Training and Interpretation of Small Models
- **Authors**: Michael Ruofan Zeng , Junaid Hasan , Xuanyu Yang , Emily Xia Meng , Henry Kvinge
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33966)
- **Abstract**:
> There are several approaches to AI-driven mathematical discovery. One of these involves the use of large language models (LLMs) to resolve well-specified conjectures via text-based reasoning, while another uses narrow models and interpretability for more open-ended, human-in-the-loop exploration using object-based mathematical data. The latter is limited in its ability to move beyond simple empirical pattern detection while the former is not well-designed for direct, open-ended exploration of large mathematical datasets. In this paper we introduce PatternMiner, a system that combines these two paradigms. In PatternMiner, an LLM agent generates mathematical datasets based on an initial broad question, trains narrow machine learning models on them, uses interpretability techniques to extract patterns from these trained models, and uses these patterns to formulate conjectures. Once an initial conjecture has been formulated, PatternMiner instructs the agent to search for counterexamples using calls to mathematical software. If a set of counterexamples can be found, these are used as feedback for conjecture refinement or another round of training and interpretability. The conjecture progressively evolves through this iterative sequence with the agent allowed to try to prove it at any point. We describe the results of applying PatternMiner to five well-known positivity problems in algebraic combinatorics. PatternMiner proves several new theorems related to Schubert positivity and projection, a character-row evaluation formula, a restricted version of the Kazhdan--Lusztig combinatorial invariance conjecture, and Schur positivity for a family of immanant coefficients. We end by analyzing the most common routes to conjecture generation that are used by PatternMiner in our case studies as well as documenting the most common failure modes.

## 19. FORK: Co-Evolving Safe Disclosure and Decision Skills Against Attackers Who Read Every Refusal
- **Authors**: Hieu Man , Chao Shang , Rui Dong , Devang Kulshreshtha , Yanjun Qi , Sandesh Swamy
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33879)
- **Abstract**:

Notably, on MT-AgentRisk, whose environments and tasks are unseen in training, FORK lowers ASR from 70.6% to 9.1% with 93.1% benign utility.
