## 1. LiveGeo: Benchmarking Geographic Agents in Continuously Evolving Worlds
- **Authors**: Pengcheng Zhou , Xuanyu Liu , Yanchen Yin , Tianjie Ju , Bobo Li , Shengqiong Wu , Hao Fei , Mong-Li Lee , Wynne Hsu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17267](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17267)
- **Abstract**:
> We introduce GeoWorld, a controllable and continuously evolving geographic environment platform for LLM agents, and LiveGeo, a benchmark for evaluating reliable decision-making in real-world geographic environments using GeoWorld. Unlike existing evaluations that largely reason and plan over fixed geographic states, GeoWorld is grounded in real-world geographic structure and allows external world states to evolve along their own temporal processes while preserving verifiable state histories. LiveGeo places geographic tasks within this evolving environment, requiring agents to actively acquire task-relevant information, verify whether the evidence supporting prior decisions remains valid, and revise their plans when necessary. Our results reveal that even frontier LLMs struggle with static tasks, with no model consistently excelling across tasks, while strong static performance does not ensure dynamic reliability. Further analysis identifies a key bottleneck: agents struggle to acquire and translate relevant evidence into valid decisions and maintain their correctness as the world evolves. By releasing LiveGeo together with its GeoWorld platform, we aim to provide the community with a flexible and extensible infrastructure for studying the next generation of agents capable of reliable action in evolving geographic worlds.

## 2. Ballad: Bandit-Based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery
- **Authors**: Samidha Verma , Ankit Anand , Sayan Ranu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17241](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17241)
- **Abstract**:
> Modern automated heuristic discovery (AHD) systems increasingly rival hand-crafted heuristics by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to iteratively mutate and refine solutions. A natural assumption is that more powerful and more expensive models consistently yield better heuristics. We challenge this empirically: across multiple NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems, we find no consistent advantage for larger, costlier models. This raises a deeper question: given a pool of LLMs spanning frontier and freely hosted or open-weight models, how should one adaptively select which model to invoke at each step of the search? The challenge is non-trivial. The optimal LLM may shift over the course of a run, and because one LLM's output seeds the context for subsequent calls, each model's reward signal is non-stationary and entangled across the heuristic population, thus violating the independence assumptions of classical online decision-making settings. We address this through Ballad (Bandit-based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery), an online bandit algorithm that learns to orchestrate a pool of LLMs into an ensemble that matches or exceeds what any soloist LLM achieves alone. This orchestration is powered by an LLM selection policy that favors rare, high-scoring heuristics over stable averages, a DAG-based credit propagation mechanism through the genealogy of the heuristic population, and discounting of stale observations to remain attuned to each model's current utility. Across five NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems and a pool of four models of varying capability and cost, Ballad not only matches the best individual LLM in hindsight but, in several cases, surpasses every individual model while reducing API cost by up to 65.6% relative to always using the strongest model.

## 3. Al-Ghasali: A Computational Framework for Simulating Islamic Jurisprudential Rulings via Finite State Automata and Large Language Models
- **Authors**: Elnaserledinellah Mahmoud Abdelwahab
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17235)
- **Abstract**:
> This paper presents Al Ghasali, a computational framework designed to formalize and simulate Islamic jurisprudential (Fiqh) rulings. Building upon recent set theoretic formalizations of al sabr wa al Taqsim that extract minimal operational rules as simple Horn clause logical programs, this work proves that the semantics of closed Fiqh chapters collapse completely to deterministic Finite State Automata (FSA). We establish a formal computational hierarchy, demonstrating that restricting formalization to finite propositional Horn theories guarantees decidability and constant time (O(1)) verdict extraction after offline compilation. The framework formally proves that hierarchical compositions of sub-chapter FSAs preserve this O(1) complexity, and that temporal juristic requirements, such as Tartib (order), are deterministically flattened into static ontological states, preserving the memoryless property of the automaton. To generate human-readable juristic summaries, Al Ghasali incorporates Large Language Models (LLMs) via an architecture where the deterministic FSA strictly controls the probabilistic LLM. Crucially, the paper formulates sufficient design safety conditions for this FSA LLM integration, ensuring strict adherence to the epistemological boundaries of Usul al Fiqh. To neutralize the risk of LLM hallucination, the system employs a Grammar Guided Constrained Decoding mechanism. We formally define the mapping function that dynamically constructs unambiguous right linear grammars from the FSA execution history. The paper mathematically proves that this architecture, augmented by a Bounded Neutral Lexicon and Valence Locking, guarantees the absolute absence of Decisional Hallucination, Juristic Factual Hallucination, and Semantic Inversion reducing the LLM to a pure linguistic transformer of deterministic juristic facts

## 4. CapLite: Prompt-Injection Defense for Small Language Model Agents without Model-Written Security Artifacts
- **Authors**: Zimo Xu , Wenzhuo Liu , Hideki Nakayama
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17115)
- **Abstract**:
> Enforcement defenses against prompt injection, such as CaMeL, Progent, IPIGuard and DRIFT, ask the agent's own model to write a security artifact (a program, a privilege policy, a dependency graph or a constraint list) that code then enforces. We ask whether small language model (SLM) agents can be trusted to write these artifacts. Across 15 open-weight models, 13 of them between 1.7B and 12B parameters, they cannot: the policies, graphs and constraints that the agent's model writes admit attacks on 14 (Progent), 13 (IPIGuard) and 11 (DRIFT) of the models, and CaMeL's programs fail to run in up to 68% of episodes. We present CapLite, which derives its policy from the tool schemas, commits the write calls before any tool result is read, and admits a write only if every argument that decides whom or what it affects is stated in the user's request. This guarantees write integrity: injected text cannot add a write, and no write reaches a recipient, amount, object or destination that the user did not state. No attack succeeds in 13,935 attacked AgentDojo episodes. CapLite reaches utility under attack comparable to CaMeL's (29.0% vs. 27.2%) with fewer tokens than every other enforcement defense and than the undefended agent: 17.1k per episode, 18% of CaMeL's, and the highest utility per token of all enforcement defenses.

## 5. Skill Adapter: Personalized Modification and Online Routing of General-Purpose Skills
- **Authors**: Qijia Zhuang , Yanyi Gou , Wenbin Ma , Yuxiang Ren
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17007)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) agents use external skills to reuse task procedures and experience with tools. General-purpose skills describe shared execution procedures, while specific tool choices, operating practices, and output requirements vary across users. In long-horizon tasks, subgoals, available inputs, and environmental states change as execution progresses. Agents must select currently applicable skills from an expanding skill pool and prepare candidates for subsequent steps. We propose Skill Adapter, which enables personalized modification and online routing of general-purpose skills. The system distills local adjustments from user requirements and execution feedback and stores them as Adapters linked to general-purpose skills. The routing agent selects general-purpose skills and applicable Adapters based on the current subtask, execution progress, and environmental feedback, incrementally maintaining a task-level online skill library that the main LLM reads on demand. The system preloads candidates for subsequent nodes and rechecks their applicability once actual inputs are determined. Reinforcement learning optimizes the two types of decisions using modification gains and routing outcomes, respectively. We evaluate Skill Adapter on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SkillsBench. Compared with SkillRL, it improves task success rates by an average of 11.43 percentage points and reduces mean execution time on all three benchmarks. Skill Adapter retains its performance advantage when the skill pool grows to 1,000 skills.

## 7. Compiling Known Rules into Compact Neural Agents: Weight-Space Programming on Unseen Games
- **Authors**: Jens Lehmann
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16981](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16981)
- **Abstract**:
> Weight-space programming (WSP) uses language models to construct neural network weights without training. We test whether known game mechanics can be compiled into compact agents that generalise to held-out levels, renderings and start states within a game family. Writers receive simulator source with held-out level data removed for 20 privately authored, unpublished ARC-AGI-3-style game families. With a fixed 57k-parameter recurrent architecture, the strongest writer reaches 56% held-out-level success with feedback, while the four main writers' Python players reach 97-100%. An exploratory free-architecture condition reaches 68-90%. Learning from support-level examples transfers much less well, but these learners lack the source information given to writers. Written networks have a program-like structure: 93% of parameters are zero, and 90% of the rest are small integers or powers of two. NumPy players under comparable computation caps reach 91%, versus the networks' 79%. Training the strongest writer's architectures from scratch yields about 1% success within our budget. Written fixed-network weights are fragile, and standard PPO removes most of their success. Prompts for noise robustness increase tolerance by about four orders of magnitude for two of three writers, but PPO makes larger updates and still removes most success. These results demonstrate rule-to-network compilation in this setting, while showing limited benefits over Python programs and no successful combination with the tested PPO fine-tuning.

## 8. S1-Evo: Experience-Guided Evidence Competition Attacks on Typed System-1 Decision Models
- **Authors**: Kaixiang Wang , Junjie Li , Zhaojiacheng Zhou , Jiong Lou , Jie Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16937](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16937)
- **Abstract**:
> Typed System-1 (S1) decision models are increasingly deployed in LLM agents for routing, moderation, and authorization to cut inference cost, yet untrusted context can still manipulate typed outputs and alter execution branches. Unlike autoregressive System-2 (S2) models, S1 interfaces score dynamic rules and options without token-by-token decoding, mismatching attacks targeting text continuation or explicit override commands. We propose S1-Evo, a self-evolving framework tailored to S1's contract-based option competition and margin feedback. S1-Evo perturbs untrusted fields via contract-aware evidence channels to induce \emph{evidence competition} with original task evidence while preserving fact invariance. Offline on seed tasks, it optimizes candidates via feedback search guided by directional margins and re-verification, distilling verified flips into experience cards. At test time, S1-Evo retrieves relevant cards to construct perturbations for unseen tasks. Across four benchmarks and three S1 models, S1-Evo reaches 71.99\% mean ASR and 57.47\% high-confidence ASR on \texttt{jev-1.14}, and causes up to 24.00\% E2E agent violations.

## 9. Scale-Induced Semantic Shift in Visual Prompt Injection Against VLMs and Web Agents
- **Authors**: Yiheng Zhao , Runze Ji , Chenyu Yi , Yi Yu , Bingquan Shen , Alex Kot , Xudong Jiang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16744)
- **Abstract**:
> Visual content in multimedia systems may be resized, compressed, or re-rendered before it is interpreted by a vision-language model. We study a security failure mode in which these transformations change the machine-readable semantics of an image: a textual instruction is not recovered at a $512\times512$ presentation resolution under our evaluation protocol but becomes recoverable after aggressive downsampling. We formulate this behavior as Scale-Induced Semantic Shift (SISS) and develop a closed-loop black-box framework that combines dual-scale verification, selective self-hardening, shallow-layer low-rank adaptation, and pixel-space noise regularization. On a fixed 300-case benchmark, the third SISS refinement round obtains a $58.75\%$ average attack success rate across four closed-source vision-language models, compared with $41.08\%$ for the strongest generation baseline. A controlled sweep bounds the effect to aggressive compression: average success decreases to $17.68\%$, $6.85\%$, and $5.60\%$ at $128\times128$, $224\times224$, and $336\times336$, respectively. In a downstream web-agent study, the generated images increase hidden-instruction perception, but absolute behavioral compromise remains about $4\%$. Generic input transformations provide little protection in a separate defense protocol, whereas the proposed ScaleGuard consistency detector reduces non-adaptive success from $61.4\%$ to $24.3\%$ and one-round defense-aware success from $42.5\%$ to $15.0\%$, with no observed rejection among matched clean controls. The results characterize a bounded but reproducible semantic inconsistency in multimedia preprocessing and motivate scale-consistent visual-language inference.

## 10. Mind the Accuracy-Approval Gap: How Users Evaluate Sycophantic AI in Everyday Tasks
- **Authors**: Wajiha Naveed , Brandon Fain
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16722)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) often exhibit sycophancy, a tendency to agree with users, flatter or affirm them, and preserve their face, even when independent judgment or critical feedback would be more useful. Prior work has largely evaluated sycophancy through benchmark evaluations, but less is known about how users perceive and respond to sycophantic AI in everyday tasks. We combine benchmark evaluations with two large-scale user studies. We study four personas defined through system prompts: objective (instructed to prioritize accuracy over agreement), soft-sycophantic, hard-sycophantic, and baseline (no persona-specific system prompt). Benchmarks show that even baseline exhibits substantial sycophancy, agreeing with users' stated views 71.7% of the time on NLP Survey, compared with 54.8% for objective and 89.5% for hard-sycophantic. Under conversational pressure on TruthfulQA, accuracy ranged from 81.9% for objective to 59.6% for baseline and 8.7% for hard-sycophantic. Participants were assigned to one of the four personas, and either discussed contested policy topics or collaborated with AI on email writing. Despite its higher accuracy under pressure, objective AI was rated significantly less respectful and satisfying than the other personas, and less trustworthy than baseline. Baseline and sycophantic personas did not differ significantly. Persona did not significantly affect how often participants changed their opinions, although participants who discussed with objective AI had greater odds of reduced confidence. Objective AI prompted greater writing expansion than soft-sycophantic AI, but final writing quality did not differ significantly across personas. These findings highlight a tension between accuracy and user approval: personas with substantially lower accuracy under conversational pressure received more favorable ratings for respect and satisfaction than objective AI.

## 11. When Expert Disagreement Hurts: Prestige and Selectivity in LLM Decision Revision
- **Authors**: Yupeng Tang , Mingfeng Lin
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16694)
- **Abstract**:
> When language models revise a decision after another agent disagrees, neither reversal counts nor final accuracy shows whether they are fixing mistakes or abandoning correct answers. We introduce a controlled two-pass audit that holds the question, the evidence, and the model's initial answer fixed and varies only the revision context: neutral reflection, anonymous disagreement, or disagreement attributed to a domain expert. For binary decisions the accounting is exact: revision raises accuracy only if it is selective enough, reversing wrong answers more than $a/(1-a)$ times as often as correct ones, where $a$ is initial accuracy (nine times at 90%). Across five models and 1,989 evidence-grounded decisions in medicine, science, and law, no model clears this bar under expert disagreement, and accuracy falls by up to 16.1 points. The expert label, a prestige cue, adds reversals for four of the five models, yet its effect on accuracy ranges from a 5.2-point loss to a 2.4-point gain, so reversal counts are a poor proxy for harm. With identical arguments from both sources, the label still raises reversal for three proprietary models and in a pooled analysis of four newer models. Models also respond to stated source reliability. Revision pipelines should therefore be evaluated with paired, conditional metrics, per model and protocol.

## 12. Safety as a Budget: Calibrating the Control Channel of Constrained GRPO for Vision-Language Models
- **Authors**: Bo Han
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16628)
- **Abstract**:
> Reinforcement learning can teach vision-language models (VLMs) to decline harmful image-text requests, but how safe the result becomes is set indirectly, through reward weights found by trial runs. Stating safety as budgets on the rate of harmful assistance and the rate of over-refusal, enforced by Lagrange multipliers inside GRPO with an off-the-shelf scorer as the cost, is the natural fix, and it fails twice. The multiplier compares the scorer's mean with the budget, and scorers built for ranking get the mean wrong: a fixed-point analysis shows that the mean bias, not the budget, then decides whether the multiplier settles, saturates or switches off. And standard GRPO standardizes the penalized reward within each group, which cancels the multiplier before it reaches the gradient. Two-channel constrained GRPO repairs both: the base reward keeps the raw score for the within-group comparison, the penalty and the constraint estimate pass through a monotone map fitted on judge-labeled training responses, and the advantage keeps the multiplier. On a Qwen3-VL-2B policy with a training-free readout of an open 4B model as the scorer, held-out harm on 1,037 adversarial prompts falls from 8.9% under standard GRPO to 4.8%, lower in every seed, where the uncalibrated budget gives 7.6%; over-refusal stays at most 2% on 400 benign queries and MMBench is unchanged. The gain is largest where the intent sits in the image (10.1% -> 5.1%) and grows on the unseen HADES benchmark (28.9% -> 12.8%). Order-preserving distortions of the scores reproduce each predicted controller state in every seed, and the results hold under a map fitted on another judge's labels, an image-reading judge and a human audit.

## 13. Large Language Models as Digital Twins: a Distribution-Aware Questionnaire-Based Evaluation
- **Authors**: Francesco Di Cursi , Chiara Boldrini , Andrea Passarella , Marco Conti
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16586)
- **Abstract**:
> This paper investigates whether Large Language Models can answer a personality questionnaire as a specific individual would. Given a user's social-media posts, the model completes the BFI-2-60 Big Five questionnaire as that user. The predicted responses are converted into trait scores and compared with the user's actual scores. We evaluate GPT-4, Gemma, Llama 3.1, and Mistral on the MyPersonality dataset. To determine whether predictions reflect information from the user's writing, we compare them with answers produced without user text and with values derived from general population tendencies. When given an explicit profile, GPT-4 most clearly translates trait information into consistent questionnaire responses, particularly for Extraversion and Neuroticism. Increasing context does not systematically reduce error: open-source models fluctuate more, whereas GPT-4 remains comparatively stable. Overall, the evaluated models do not consistently reproduce the questionnaire behavior of specific individuals.

## 14. AI has its own style in mathematical proving
- **Authors**: Xiaokai Li , Ziyu Zhou , Qibin Cheng , Yangcheng Wu , An Zeng , Ying Fan
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16527)
- **Abstract**:
> As large language models increasingly generate mathematical proofs, whether they develop a proving style distinct from that of human mathematicians remains an open question. This question is particularly pressing as mathematicians increasingly voice concerns that proofs generated by large language models are difficult to interpret. Here we ask three large language models to independently reprove all 465 propositions of Euclid's \textit{Elements} and compare their citation choices against the human-proof baseline through Jaccard similarity and complex network analysis. We find that AI-generated proofs differ from human proofs, and this difference is stable and reproducible: mean pairwise Jaccard similarity among AI runs (~0.68) is nearly twice that between AI and human proofs (~0.37), and cross-run classification consistency is also high. By examining citation structure in detail, we identify the concrete signature of this difference: approximately 40% of AI citations point to foundational elements (definitions, common notions, and postulates), compared with only 13\% in human proofs; even among theorem-to-theorem citations, AI tends to cite more distant results. Roughly half of AI's extra citations trace to the upstream dependencies of the human proof. Together, these findings suggest that the AI proving style is a tendency to construct arguments on its own from foundational elements rather than directly citing later, more established results, which we term "building from the ground up". Our findings offer a new perspective on how AI systems organise and invoke mathematical knowledge, with implications for understanding the interpretability of AI-generated proofs.

## 15. Lost in Delegation: Studying the Effectiveness of Task Orchestration in Generative Search
- **Authors**: Tamara Stojanovska , Elisabeth Kirsten , Leon Swazinna , Muhammad Bilal Zafar
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16526)
- **Abstract**:
> LLMs have quickly evolved from models used in isolation to agentic systems that delegate tasks to external tools and other LLMs to solve complex assignments. This agentic augmentation has led to impressive capability gains. However, when and how delegation improves performance remains poorly understood. For example, it is unclear whether delegation helps when tasks involve post-cutoff information that the orchestrating LLM cannot reliably resolve from its own knowledge. In this work, we use purpose-built datasets with pre- and post-cutoff splits to compare various delegation modes. We find that delegation provides no consistent accuracy improvement over non-delegated setups, with effects varying across models and tasks. When tasks involve knowledge unavailable to the orchestrator, delegation can substantially degrade accuracy, while direct tool use with the same LLM performs much better. Our analysis identifies distorted delegated queries, inefficient tool use, and failures to preserve and use information between agents as recurring failure modes. These findings suggest that effective orchestration requires both well-calibrated delegation decisions and effective exchange of information between agents.

## 16. SignRot: LLM Quantization with Massive Outlier-Aware Sign-Adjusted Rotation
- **Authors**: Jaehun Gim , Jaewoo Kim , Gyuwan Kim , Seo Yeon Park
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16502)
- **Abstract**:
> While quantization compresses LLMs and accelerates inference, outlier activations impede efficient low-bit representation. In particular, massive outliers cause substantial performance degradation. Although rotation-based methods that rely on randomized Hadamard transforms for online inference aim to mitigate this, they can produce fragmented distributions with empty bins when a massive outlier dominates a token's activation. This fragmentation severely undermines uniform quantization performance. Moreover, massive outliers concentrate in the down-projection input, where the preceding nonlinearity requires the rotation to be applied at inference time. This constraint prevents training-based approaches such as SpinQuant from learning the online rotation at this position, as a learned dense matrix cannot use the Fast Walsh-Hadamard Transform and would require a full matrix multiplication during inference. To address this, we propose SignRot (Sign-adjusted Rotation), which identifies massive outlier channels and constructs a sign adjustment vector that unifies the signs of the corresponding Hadamard row, collapsing the bimodal distribution into a unimodal profile amenable to quantization. Since the aligned outlier shifts the activation mean away from zero, we pair SignRot with asymmetric activation quantization, whose zero-point absorbs this shift. As a lightweight, drop-in improvement compatible with any Hadamard-based quantization method, SignRot requires only a single calibration forward pass and adds only element-wise sign flips at inference, without an additional matrix multiplication. Applying SignRot to QuaRot achieves a WikiText2 perplexity of 5.88 and an average zero-shot reasoning accuracy of 72.73, surpassing SpinQuant on the LLaMA3-70B model. Code is available at this https URL .

## 17. Recalibrating a Typed-Decision Model in an Afternoon: A Hands-On Recipe for Laya's Confidence Scores
- **Authors**: Mohammed Nayeem Khan , Aliyas Ahmad Misri
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16499)
- **Abstract**:
> A typed-decision model such as Laya reads a text and a list of options that the caller defines, for example ticket categories, and returns a probability for each option. Code around it compares the top probability with a threshold to answer, abstain or escalate to a person. Earlier work reports, for a Turkish model, that temperature scaling can raise held-out calibration error (ECE). This tutorial gives a five-step recipe (measure, diagnose, fit, validate, deploy) in 130 lines of numpy with standard methods, none new. On 1,065 synthetic, LLM-labelled items the released Laya checkpoint has a pooled ECE of 0.073 but a bucket-macro ECE of 0.143 (0.127-0.184), the mean over buckets (questions grouped by type and option count): the 12-option bucket is over-confident by +0.22, the three-to-five-option bucket under-confident by 0.05, and they cancel. Confidence-only maps (Platt, histogram, isotonic) need 20 or more labelled items per bucket to approach the temperature; with 320 per bucket the lowest macro-ECE belongs to Platt. In these files the retrained checkpoints store no per-bucket table and the stored 12-option value 0.1006 is clamped to 0.5 at runtime; the refit helps 31 of 33 checkpoints on random folds and 21 with a set held out. Fit and test share one generator and LLM labels, so these are findings about these checkpoints and this data.

## 18. How many LLM-labelled rows does a decision encoder need, and how wrong can the labels be?
- **Authors**: Mohammed Nayeem Khan , Aliyas Ahmad Misri
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16498](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16498)
- **Abstract**:
> A decision encoder such as Laya reads a question with named options (for example, which department should handle a ticket) and a text, and picks an option. It reads both in one forward pass and returns a probability for every option, without writing any text. We ask how many LLM-written training rows such an encoder needs and how wrong those labels may be. We ran 19 fine-tunes of Laya (ModernBERT-large) on nested subsets of a 2,818-row pool and scored them on 1,065 held-out items from the same generator, so every test item is in-distribution and no out-of-family test was run. Accuracy rises from 69.4% before fine-tuning to 74.3% with 5% of the rows, 84.8% with 50% and 86.9% with all rows. The curve flattens, and a saturating fit with bootstrap intervals cannot pin down its plateau. Seed spread at 20% of the rows (s.d. 1.6 points) exceeds some of the steps between sizes. Giving small subsets as many updates as the full pool gains 1.9 points at 5% and 1.9 points at 20% of the rows (one seed; p=0.14 and 0.13), and at 20% that is below the 2.3-point difference between two ordinary seeds. A single run with 10% symmetric label noise shows no detectable change (-0.2 points, 95% interval -1.7 to +1.4); 20% costs 3.4 points and 40% costs 8.7 points. Confusable-pair noise at 20% costs 7.4 points, and under noise the model moves from over-confident to under-confident. An independent labeller disagrees with the gold labels on 3.5% of held-out items, and about half of its choice disagreements (16 of 33, leaving each item out of the partner map) name the partner option that the confusable-pair noise uses. Laya is +0.9 points ahead of a TF-IDF classifier trained on the same rows at full size (the interval includes zero) and +21.1 points ahead at 5% of the rows. Everything is synthetic, LLM-labelled and from one backbone, one trainer and mostly one seed.

## 19. One Model, Two Precisions: Does Disagreement Between Quantized Copies Tell You When a Small Model Is Wrong?
- **Authors**: Mohammed Nayeem Khan , Allan Kevin T
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16478](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16478)
- **Abstract**:
> Local language models often exist at several precisions, and quantization is known to change individual answers. This study asks whether the disagreement between two precisions of the same weights, an 8-bit copy that answers and a lower-precision copy that reads the same prompt, works as a per-question error score for abstention, escalation and re-asking.
