## 1. Charts as Epistemic Actions for Experience-Driven Agentic Data Analysis
- **Authors**: Xinzhe Wang , Xudong Weng , Zang Li , Shaolei Zhang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30072)
- **Abstract**:
> Data agents built on ReAct-style loops now execute complex analytical workflows over structured data, yet their intermediate reasoning still runs almost entirely through text. Human analysts instead move between numbers, tables, and charts, reading visual evidence to expose trends and anomalies that text summaries drop. We ask whether charts can serve as internal epistemic actions inside a data agent's reasoning loop, and how to teach an agent to use them. We first build \probe{}, a suite of 200 controlled tasks under four matched interface conditions, and find a persistent gap between what agents can read and what they choose to use. Agents score as well from self-generated charts as from numeric output, yet plotting access alone does not translate into consistent use. Task slices show that charts lead on temporal, pattern, and anomaly reasoning, while text leads on precise reading and conditional verification. We then propose \method{}, which distils two families of experience from conditioned trajectory rollouts. One family records how to select and verify analytical operations, and the other records when to invoke text, charts, or both. A trajectory judge scores outcomes and labels intermediate steps, and a validation gate admits only experience that improves held-out performance. On three held-out benchmarks with four LLM backbones, \method{} raises analytical accuracy and visual evidence quality over matched baselines, and backbones that never drew charts begin using them, all without touching model parameters.

## 2. TIDE: Tree-Induced Fine-Grained Diagnostic Evaluation from Model Behaviors
- **Authors**: Jiayi Chen , Hui Li , Yujiong Shen , Qiyuan Peng , Yanxin Li , Chenhao Huang , Shaofan Liu , Guoqiang Zhang , Junjie Ye , Shihan Dou , Ming Zhang , Maxm Pan , Tao Gui , Qi Zhang , Xuanjing Huang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29985)
- **Abstract**:
> Benchmark performance is the dominant standard for comparing large language models (LLMs), typically summarized by a single aggregate score, such as accuracy, pass rate, or rubric score. This aggregate score provides limited information about a model's underlying capabilities, yet understanding these capabilities is crucial for model development and evaluation. To this end, we propose Tree-Induced Diagnostic Evaluation (TIDE), which recasts diagnostic evaluation as behavior-driven decision-tree induction to perform fine-grained capability diagnosis. TIDE recursively builds a hierarchical capability tree from benchmark samples and model scores: at each node, an LLM judge proposes candidate splits and performs sample-to-group classification, and behavior-based information gain selects among them. We apply TIDE to five models across four benchmarks to demonstrate its effectiveness, and find fine-grained capability contrasts invisible at the aggregate level: Claude Opus 4.6 achieves 100% on microeconomic market reasoning where GPT-5.4 scores 83.3%, but trails GPT-5.4 on contract law reasoning with 77.8% versus 88.9%. We hope TIDE can be extended to past and future benchmarks so that the rich behavioral signal in evaluation results is fully leveraged for understanding model capabilities.

## 3. Commit or Abstain? System One Decision Models, LLMs, and Missing Legal Authority
- **Authors**: M. Mikail Demir
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29961)
- **Abstract**:
> Given the enormous number of claims to be checked, verifiable legal AI requires scalable, cost-effective models that answer the practical needs of law. System One decision models are built on large language models but, instead of generating text, return a calibrated probability for each option. This suits the verification of legal authority, where the question is closed: a given holding, or none of them. We test whether such models abstain when the correct authority is missing. We extend CaseHOLD with an abstention condition in which the correct holding is replaced in 1,670 of 5,314 items, and compare four decision models with six generative LLMs. When no option is correct, generative LLMs still pick one in 38-86% of items; the most cautious decision model does so in 30%. On standard CaseHOLD, a decision model matches the best generative LLM (88.1% vs. 88.2%) at 1/55 of the cost. Accepting that LLM's answer only when a decision model agrees halves its wrong citations, and distilling a decision model's probabilities into Qwen3-8B, without labels, yields a well-calibrated local model. Legal AI should be judged by its wrong citations, not by accuracy alone.

## 4. ToM2Vec: Continuous Theory-of-Mind Tokens for Multi-Agent Coordination
- **Authors**: Nithin Sivakumaran , Ali Payani , Hyunji Lee , Mohit Bansal , Elias Stengel-Eskin
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29934)
- **Abstract**:
> Forming an accurate theory-of-mind (ToM) is key to successful multi-agent collaboration and requires reasoning under uncertainty to capture multiple possible partner states. This is hard to do in the text representation space native to large language model (LLM) agents, which more easily describe a single partner state. To address this challenge, we introduce ToM2Vec, which learns to form abstract theories-of-mind in a rich latent space via a fixed budget of continuous reasoning tokens. During training, supervision for these tokens comes from the partner's actual thoughts, which the ToM tokens are trained to abstract and match. At test-time, ToM tokens are generated by each agent and act as conditioners for that agent's thoughts and actions. Across several environments covering collaborative, competitive, and mixed-motive settings (Overcooked-AI, multiparty negotiation, Decrypto, Hanabi, and Collab Escape/Capture), we evaluate ToM2Vec's ability to support multi-agent interactions and collaboration, finding consistent improvements over the base model and a variety of competitive trained and prompted baselines. We show that latent reasoning is critical, with performance dropping when reasoning is expressed in text. Moreover, we show that ToM2Vec training generalizes to partner model families not seen at train-time, including a proprietary model (GPT-5.4). In our analysis, we provide evidence that the latent tokens encode a ToM, rather than task-specific information: ToM2Vec's tokens improve the agent's ability to directly answer questions about partner beliefs, desires, and next actions.

## 5. Real PMIDs, Wrong Papers: Harness Design for Biomedical Claim-Verification Agents
- **Authors**: Mohammed Saqlain
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29812)
- **Abstract**:
> Literature-searching language agents are becoming a standard component of scientific assistants for the life sciences, yet there is little controlled evidence on which parts of their harness matter. We run a controlled ablation on biomedical claim verification (SciFact) with the live PubMed API as the only tool. Holding the backbone and output format fixed, we compare four harnesses: closed-book answering, single-shot retrieval using the claim as the query, a ReAct-style agent that writes its own PubMed queries, and an oracle given the gold abstracts, each with two efficient frontier backbones. Naive single-shot retrieval finds the gold paper for only 6% of claims and turns overconfident errors into abstentions without improving accuracy. Letting the model formulate its own queries triples gold recall and yields the best verdicts (62% accuracy vs. 49-59% for the other realistic harnesses), although these differences are not significant at our sample size and agents use fewer than half of their search budget. With gold abstracts, the same models reach 79-86% accuracy, a significant 17-24 point gap showing that retrieval, not reasoning, is the main bottleneck. Finally, a citation audit shows that closed-book models cite PubMed IDs that almost always exist (327/335) but are almost never relevant: a cross-family LLM judge rates only 3 of 327 existing citations as relevant to the claim, versus 67% for citations produced by the search agent. Existence checks are therefore insufficient safeguards for scientific agents. We release code, prompts, and all cached tool calls at this https URL .

## 6. TraceForge: Distilling with Your Own Thinking
- **Authors**: Yan Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29680)
- **Abstract**:
> Using an LLM to generate training data for another is among the most general ways to train one, and it remains common practice. Some frontier teams have closed that route for their own models: what comes back is an answer, or a short summary, without the detailed reasoning trace behind it. That is a little ironic: they were themselves built on distilling human knowledge, and distilling an open-source model is still entirely routine; the restriction applies to their own outputs alone. Recent work recovers the hidden reasoning by replaying encrypted blocks across models. It somehow works, but it breaks the providers' terms of service, so it is not something a product can be built on long-term. We built the alternative. We take the context and the teacher's answer as input, and generate the reasoning trace ourselves, in the style of the student's own model family. Training is two-stage, supervised and then adversarial: a generator learns to regenerate the family's own reasoning traces from their context and answer, and a critic learns how real a reasoning trace looks. Applied to a teacher's answer, the generator writes one in the family's style. An independent judge rates how likely traces are to be genuine on a scale from 0 to 1: ours average 0.68, real traces 0.66, and traces selected by the critic from four candidates 0.82. These are authenticity scores, not correctness rates. We test the idea on five benchmarks with everything else held fixed. On general tasks the five training corpora are about the same; on coding, the generated reasoning beats Fable 5's own thinking content. With the same 5,000 questions and the same teacher answers, HumanEval+ improves by 3.7 points and MBPP+ by 7.1 at the final checkpoint, and the MBPP+ lead holds at every checkpoint. We suspect that reasoning written in the student's family style is easier for it to learn from.

## 7. Parallel Thinking for Large Language Model with Path Diversity
- **Authors**: Jiaru Qian , Yihong Dong , Xue Jiang , Chun-Wah Hsu , Zhi Jin , Ge Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29501)
- **Abstract**:
> Parallel thinking has emerged as a promising paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) by exploring multiple reasoning paths simultaneously. However, we identify that parallel thinking via Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically suffers from the mode collapse of reasoning paths, where parallel thinking trajectories degenerate into similar ones. This homogeneity undermines the fundamental motivation of parallel thinking, that is, maintaining multiple hypotheses concurrently to prevent premature loss into a suboptimal solution. In this paper, we propose Diverse Parallel Thinking (DPT), a simple yet effective RL approach for parallel thinking designed to encourage path diversity. DPT rewards the model to develop distinct reasoning strategies by penalizing the prefix similarity of parallel thinking paths, thereby enhancing a more comprehensive exploration of the reasoning space. We conduct various experiments and analyses across multiple reasoning benchmarks to demonstrate the effectiveness of DPT. Results show that DPT successfully outperforms previous parallel thinking methods, achieving state-of-the-art performance over baselines. We validate path diversity as a key element to improve parallel thinking. Our code is available at this https URL .

## 8. From Prompts to Posteriors: Can LLM Agents Perform Bayesian Inference in Context?
- **Authors**: Linus Filbry , Luckeciano C. Melo , Yarin Gal
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29331)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied across the scientific discovery pipeline, from synthesizing literature to generating hypotheses to informing experimental design. A unifying principle behind these tasks is the need to continuously update beliefs as evidence accumulates, for which Bayesian inference provides a natural framework. However, existing work assumes that LLMs cannot carry out principled Bayesian belief updating in context, instead relying on simplifications and heuristics. To challenge this assumption, we present a principled evaluation of Bayesian reasoning capabilities in LLMs, analyzing both the model's internal consistency as well as its ability to recover an externally determined posterior. Specifically, we build an evaluation framework spanning qualitative epistemological examples to probe probabilistic coherence, quantitative benchmarks of Bayesian inference capabilities, and analyses of the impact of non-Bayesian updates on downstream task performance. Across different scenarios, we find that LLMs generally violate Bayes' rule when updating their beliefs in context. However, when equipped with modern reasoning abilities and code execution capabilities, their performance drastically improves, enabling even the close approximation of analytically intractable posteriors. Our evaluation shows that current LLMs with access to appropriate tools could already be used to make complex belief updates in scientific discovery, enabling rapid acceleration of the efficiency and scale of AI-powered scientific discovery.

## 10. LLM-Based Exogenous World Modeling for Dynamic Scheduling
- **Authors**: Ni Zhang , Haofeng Yuan , Jianan Zhou , Shiji Song , Zhiguang Cao
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29308](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29308)
- **Abstract**:
> Dynamic scheduling requires dispatching online arrivals while anticipating future jobs, as poor decisions can compound downstream costs under nonlinear tardiness objectives. We show that a pretrained large language model (LLM) can serve as the exogenous component of a structured world model, forecasting uncertain future job arrivals. Specifically, we exploit an afterstate decomposition, which separates known deterministic shop transitions from uncertain exogenous job arrivals, enabling the LLM to forecast only the latter through a compact belief representation. Across diverse arrival distributions, AWMP reduces weighted tardiness by 6-21% on dynamic job-shop and 22-46% on flexible job-shop benchmarks, relative to the best of eight classical priority-dispatching rules.

- **Authors**: Yuyao Zhang , Yakun Cui , Han Zhu , Jinsong Lu , Yike Guo , Sirui Han
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29294](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29294)

## 13. CrashGym: Measuring the Decision Value of Experimentation for LLM Agents in Counterfactual Market Crashes
- **Authors**: Haocheng Lu , Haoxuan Cheng , Mingyang Chen , Minjun Zhu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29245)
- **Abstract**:
> Language-model agents are increasingly asked to run experiments, yet benchmarks rarely measure whether those experiments improve the decisions they are meant to inform. We introduce CrashGym, a deterministic, replayable limit-order-book simulator in which an agent must choose between a deleveraging intervention and a liquidity backstop for a market about to suffer a shock-triggered crash. Before committing, the agent may query intervention experiments in a laboratory realization of the market. Its choice is then scored on an independently seeded target realization in which the simulator executes every feasible intervention, so regret relative to the best intervention is computed by directly simulating every alternative on the same target repeats, with no human or model-based judging. The simulator implements each amplification channel---persistent price impact, liquidity withdrawal, margin-driven liquidation, and an evolving strategy mix---as an explicit mechanism that can be switched on in stages. Prospective comparisons for two further deployments are incomplete; in a separate exploratory study, mean regret is lower with laboratory access for all four deployments evaluated. A laboratory whose strategy mix evolves endogenously yields no established advantage over one with a fixed mix. CrashGym thus measures the value of experimentation as avoided decision loss and lets simulator detail be judged by the decisions it supports.

## 14. TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimization for On-Policy Self-Distillation
- **Authors**: Linfei Ji (1), Jiayun Li (2), Haozhao Wang (1), Siyu Jiang (1), Zehao Wang (3), Longtao Xiao (1), Yu-Jie Yuan (4), Ruixuan Li (1) ((1) Huazhong University of Science and Technology, (2) Tsinghua University, (3) Harbin Institute of Technology, (4) Huawei Technologies Ltd.)
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29167](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29167)
- **Abstract**:
> On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables a language model to learn from a Teacher instantiated from the same model but given access to privileged information, such as a reference solution. We find that access to sufficient privileged information allows the Teacher to generate highly accurate trajectories; however, accuracy alone does not guarantee that these trajectories are suitable for Student learning. Direct access to a complete solution may encourage shortened, reference-conditioned derivations that omit the exploration, verification, and self-correction required to solve problems without privileged information. We introduce TeachBack, a bidirectional preference-learning framework for on-policy self-distillation that learns not only from the Teacher but also how the Teacher should teach. TeachBack shapes the Teacher's generation behavior by controlling the length of the reference-reasoning suffix injected into the prompt and by varying the prompt style. While maintaining comparable Teacher accuracy, it encourages the Teacher to explore and re-solve problems, thereby reducing its reliance on the reference solution. At each rollout refresh, the shared policy generates both a privileged Teacher trajectory and a problem-only Student trajectory. A pairwise preference objective uses privileged Teacher trajectories to improve Student learning, while the reversed preference uses the Student's behavior to shape future Teacher supervision under the privileged condition. Experiments on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks show that TeachBack consistently improves Student performance over existing OPSD methods and related baselines.

## 15. Steering Black-Box Large Language Model Agents with Pitfall Anticipation World Model
- **Authors**: Jiongxiao Wang , Han Ding , Youzhi Luo , Yawei Wang , Yun Zhou , Linbo Liu , Panpan Xu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29139)
- **Abstract**:
> Frontier black-box Large Language Model (LLM) agents are proficient at long-horizon reasoning and precise action generation. However, when it comes to a specific agentic task, they have no internalized knowledge of how that particular environment unfolds. A world model offers a natural remedy, providing the agent with environment specific knowledge by foreseeing how the environment will respond to the action it is about to take. Prior world models for LLM agents mainly predict next observations at each step, which is costly and often uninformative because individual observations provide limited task-relevant guidance and not every step requires call for foresight. PAWM is trained in two stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on synthetic data labeled with agent pitfalls, followed by GRPO co-training with a black-box LLM to further optimize it in the target environment. PAWM improves Claude Sonnet 4.5 performance by 24.3% on ScienceWorld and 7.7% on WebArena-Lite, outperforming a range of baselines. These results demonstrate that PAWM provides an effective form of world model guidance for black-box LLM agents.

## 17. Same Answer, Different Reasons: Trace-Level Divergence in Multilingual Large Language Models
- **Authors**: Rui Sun , Peizhao Mei , Xiewei Cheng , Kaizhuo Chen , Taoran Li , Zhiyuan Yu , Varun Chandrasekaran
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29092)
- **Abstract**:
> Multilingual large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where visible reasoning traces support verification, education, and safety auditing, yet standard evaluations typically measure only final-answer agreement or holistic response similarity. As a result, they establish outcome consistency without determining whether users across languages receive comparable supporting evidence. We introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares visible reasoning traces as structured sequences, decomposing agreement into structural similarity, order consistency, and holistic similarity, with pair-specific calibration to enable comparison across languages. Across three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs, the natural baseline---embedding each whole response and comparing the two by cosine---systematically overstates agreement: $52.1\%$--$67.3\%$ of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not in the top quartile of structural similarity. This dissociation is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments align with structural similarity rather than with the holistic baseline. A same-language control shows that the level of divergence is language-specific: two English traces for paraphrases of the same question agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages. Our results show that answer-level and holistic evaluations can substantially overestimate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

## 18. Does Every Instance Count Equally? Order Effects in LLM Multi-Instance Processing
- **Authors**: Jingxuan Chen , Mohammad Taher Pilehvar , Jose Camacho-Collados
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29057)
- **Abstract**:
> Users increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to analyse collections of independent inputs, such as product reviews or social media posts, and return a single aggregated answer. This setting requires multi-instance processing (MIP), where a model combines information from multiple instances into one prediction. We study a task in which the model must infer a binary label for each instance, and identify the most frequent label among the full instance set. The correct answer should be invariant to instance order. By evaluating fifteen LLMs across five datasets and seven instance order patterns, with each instance set held fixed across patterns, we find substantial order sensitivity. These results show that instance order can systematically bias aggregated predictions, highlighting the need to evaluate MIP across different input arrangements.

## 85. First Passage Attention
- **Authors**: Yuxuan Liu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22714)
- **Abstract**:
> Augmenting softmax attention with forget gates typically relies on summing decay contributions along the key--query path. Because such accumulation is monotonic, these mechanisms cannot selectively erase intermediate context while preserving older, structurally relevant tokens.
