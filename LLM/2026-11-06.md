## 1. SwarmEvolve: Latent Agent Swarms for Evolutionary Discovery and Optimization
- **Authors**: Qile Jiang , George Em Karniadakis
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08483](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08483)
- **Abstract**:
> LLM-guided evolutionary search uses AI agents to improve programs for mathematical and computational problems by iteratively refining solutions and combining high-scoring methods. However, the process is often slow because the LLM generates a long textual chain of thought for each round and must repeat this generation many times to maintain population diversity. We present SwarmEvolve, a framework in which hundreds of low-cost agents per round work in swarms, reason in latent space without decoding to text, and share information by passing KV caches. Each swarm studies the problem from a different angle. A novelty filter keeps the most distinct agent ideas by directly measuring the distances between the agents' hidden state vectors. Crossover of ideas happens naturally in latent space, as a summarizer agent receives the combined KV caches of all swarm members. On 21 problems in mathematical discovery and scientific computing, SwarmEvolve gets the highest average score and needs a median of only 3 to 8 minutes to reach the best scores that other state-of-the-art frameworks reach in 60 minutes. On several problems, SwarmEvolve with Qwen3 8B improves on the solutions that AlphaEvolve first found with frontier LLMs. Our work shows that agent swarms that communicate in latent space offer a fast, low-cost approach to program search for scientific problems.

## 2. The Power of Meta Access in the Software of the Future --- Demonstrated on LLM Chat UIs and Chat AI
- **Authors**: Julian Kunkel , Ali Doosthosseini
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08428)
- **Abstract**:
> Chat remains the default surface for many large language model (LLM) tasks, even as agentic tools such as OpenCode excel at coding and automation. Yet the model sees the current dialogue but not the workspace around it. We explore meta access---giving the assistant a structured, permissioned view of the interface itself and propose a ladder of levels: feature tutoring; metadata, settings, and preference access; retrieval over summaries and messages, confirmed mutation, and full orchestration. Meta access can turn a chat client from many screens into one entry point for finding, navigating, organising, and operating it, while raising risks around grounding, privacy, prompt injection, and trust. General-purpose agentic harnesses are runtimes; because they can change their own settings, meta access becomes reflexive and needs a separate control-plane boundary. We ground this in the Meta Chat prototype of the Chat AI client. It builds bounded context from, e.g., summaries, settings, and relevant messages; links references to conversations, topics, and personas; supports confirmed, reversible changes; offers a browsable tutorial library; and recommends personas as user-started conversations. By exposing application state in structured form, meta access can extend screen readers from widget descriptions to semantic workspace operations. Meta access thus turns chat from a window onto one conversation into a control layer for the whole workspace, extending chat toward agentic capability without surrendering user control. As assistants become embedded in IDEs, dashboards, office suites, and operating systems, AI-era interfaces should expose a structured, permissioned model of their state and capabilities, ideally by opening their existing metamodels through a debugger-like interface.

## 3. Benchmarks as Software: Terminal-Bench and the Case for Continuous Benchmarks
- **Authors**: E. Kelly Buchanan , Crystal Zhou , Scott W. Linderman , Christopher Ré
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08417)
- **Abstract**:
> Coding agents are among the most economically consequential deployments of LLMs to date, making reliable evaluation increasingly important. Yet even benchmarks that are carefully validated at release can become stale. As models improve, they can discover valid solutions that existing verifiers reject, while changes to evaluation infrastructure can invalidate assumptions embedded in tasks and tests. We call the resulting accumulation of defects benchmark maintenance debt: a growing gap between benchmark scores and the capabilities they are intended to measure. We introduce a continuous-validation workflow for agentic benchmarks that combines community reports, task artifacts, agent traces, automated oracle checks, and human-gated repairs. We apply this workflow to Terminal-Bench 2.0, a benchmark that underwent extensive human and LM-assisted review and red-teaming before release. Our audit identifies issues in roughly 30% of the benchmark, and we release the resulting repairs as Terminal-Bench 2.1. These repairs change evaluation results: accuracy increased by over 10 percentage points and leaderboard positions move by up to three ranks, even as the overall ranking remains highly correlated (Spearman rho = 0.958). We evaluate how resource allocation shapes benchmark conclusions. We show that fixed wall-clock can materially change agent performance: For example, extending the time limit closes a 20pp harness gap on GPT 5.4 and shrinks a 30 pp gap on GPT 5.4 mini to 5 pp. Benchmark maintenance is therefore not only about repairing task artifacts: resource budgets are part of the measurement specification and must be validated alongside the tasks themselves. More broadly, benchmark maintenance debt is not just accumulated implementation bugs: our results show that as models improve, both evaluation assumptions and resource settings may need to be revalidated.

## 4. Benchmarking Large Language Models on Heterogeneous Cognitive and Biomedical Knowledge Graphs: The Role of Graph Grounding in Multi-Hop Reasoning and Hallucination Mitigation
- **Authors**: Ali Sarabadani , Kheirolah Rahsepar Fard , Tahereh Jeirani
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08315)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in biomedical and cognitive neuroscience, but their ability to reason reliably over heterogeneous knowledge graphs (KGs) remains unclear. We benchmark five LLMs (GPT-5.1, Gemini 2.0 Pro, Llama 3, Qwen, and BioGPT) across five KGs: DrugBank, NeuroSynth, Cognitive Atlas, Brain Map, and GDPKG. We introduce a multitier query dataset to evaluate reasoning depth and factual accuracy under KG-aware (graph-grounded) and KG-agnostic conditions. Inter-annotator agreement was substantial, with Cohen's kappa of 0.781 (range: 0.753-0.807) and Fleiss' kappa of 0.77. Results show that general-purpose models, particularly GPT-5.1 and Gemini 2.0 Pro, generally outperform domain-specific models on multi-hop and high-level reasoning tasks, although performance declines as query complexity increases. KG grounding reduces hallucination rates across all evaluated models. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, domain-specific models do not consistently outperform general-purpose models on specialized tasks without graph grounding. These findings highlight the value of combining broad language-model capabilities with structured domain knowledge. We provide practical guidance for selecting LLM-KG integration strategies based on task complexity and computational constraints, and discuss hybrid neurosymbolic architectures for personalized medicine, cognitive disorder diagnosis, and explainable AI.

## 5. RM-VLM: From Natural-Language Task Descriptions to Vision-Grounded Reward Machines
- **Authors**: Roko Parac , Christos Ziakas , Leo Ardon , Alessandra Russo
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08308](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08308)
- **Abstract**:
> Reward machines (RMs) have been proposed as a reward formulation in reinforcement learning (RL) to capture non-Markovian tasks where credit assignment depends on history. By representing reward functions as automata, RMs expose the task's temporal structure and allow such rewards to be handled within a Markovian framework. However, they assume a labelling function, a proposition-grounding oracle for observations, which must be manually engineered for each environment, limiting their wider applicability. In this work, we address this limitation by proposing RM-VLM, a novel approach in which a large language model (LLM) generates an RM from a natural-language task description, and a vision-language model (VLM) acts as the labelling function, grounding the RM's propositions over image-based observations during RM-guided policy learning. A VLM-based value function estimator is used to assess the agent's progress towards each RM transition and derive a dense reward signal. We evaluate RM-VLM on three domains covering non-Markovian grid-world navigation, robotic manipulation, and 3D object collection. Our experimental results show that RM-VLM consistently achieves higher task success rates than existing VLM-based reward methods across all tested domains.

## 7. The Geometry of Simulated Perspectives: Dissociating Representation and Behaviour in a Multimodal Language Model
- **Authors**: Mathis Immertreu , Fitim Abdullahu , Thomas Kinfe , Claus Metzner , Helmut Grabner , Patrick Krauss , Achim Schilling
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08262](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08262)
- **Abstract**:
> Multimodal language models can simulate different observers, yet it remains unclear how assigned perspectives reshape their internal representations and whether these changes govern their judgements. Here, we investigate the representational geometry of persona-conditioned visual interestingness in Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. Across 100 images and 53 prompting conditions, we systematically vary persona content and the linguistic framing used to introduce it, while recording behavioural ratings and layer-wise hidden states. We observe a systematic reorganisation of representational geometry across processing depth: early layers primarily encode image identity, whereas persona-specific structures emerge in later layers. ## 10. DictLLM: Post-training Compression of Large Language Model with Dictionary Kernels
- **Authors**: Jinho Park , Se Young Chun , Mingoo Seok
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08082](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08082)
- **Abstract**:
> Efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) on mobile devices requires balancing performance with compute and memory constraints. Post-training compression is an effective approach to managing this trade-off, while requiring much less computation than in-training counterparts. We propose DictLLM, a dictionary-based compression scheme in which weight matrices within grouped layers are decomposed into a shared dictionary and layer-specific sparse coefficients, directly obtained from a pre-trained model without further training. Specifically, we cast DictLLM as a four-step post-training optimization. First, we obtain shared dictionaries via singular value decomposition (SVD) of input-scaled weight matrices, amortizing rank across grouped layers; second, we sparsify the layer-specific coefficients to the target compression ratio using a dictionary-aware Hessian; and third, we refine the dictionaries given the resulting sparse coefficients. Finally, we aggressively quantize both components using factor-specific Hessian information: input covariance for the dictionaries and the dictionary-aware Hessian for the coefficients. The resulting representation reduces memory footprint by storing a single shared dictionary per layer group, along with sparse layer-specific coefficients. On language modeling and zero-shot reasoning benchmarks, DictLLM consistently outperforms existing post-training low-rank compression methods and pushes the Pareto frontier between model size and performance. DictLLM compresses the original LLaMA-7B model by 5.1x with only a 2.9-point increase in WikiText-2 perplexity, achieving a 3.6x higher compression ratio than the state-of-the-art low-rank method.

## 12. Circe: Training-Free LLM Self-Evaluation via Component Interaction Consistency
- **Authors**: Shuyi Miao , Zheng Li , Xu Xie , Linrui Xu , Fei Shen , Xiaoyu Du , Tat-Seng Chua
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07948)
- **Abstract**:
> As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, evaluating the correctness of their responses is crucial for reliable use. However, such evaluation is challenging because reference answers are rarely available in practice. Existing reference-free methods mainly rely on output signals, which may assign high scores to incorrect responses and thus do not necessarily reflect correctness. This motivates exploring the model's internal computation for complementary correctness signals. Recent studies exploit internal states, but they mainly analyze hidden-state trajectories across layers, which record only the combined updates of attention and feed-forward networks (FFNs) and overlook how these components interact. We therefore propose Circe, a training-free self-evaluation method that assesses a response using both internal and external signals, without reference answers or repeated sampling. For internal signals, we first introduce component interaction consistency (CIC), which measures the directional alignment between attention updates in earlier layers and attention-induced FFN output changes in later layers. We further incorporate representation geometry (RG) to characterize normalized displacement and angular changes along layerwise hidden-state trajectories. For the external signal, we convert the mean negative log-likelihood of generated tokens into a bounded likelihood confidence (LC) score. Finally, we fuse CIC, RG, and LC into a unified correctness score. Experiments on five LLMs and six benchmarks across four domains show that our method distinguishes correct from incorrect responses more effectively than existing baselines, and generalizes to multilingual, multimodal, and open-world settings.

## 14. When Bigger Becomes Brittle: Why Compression Hurts More as Language Models Scale
- **Authors**: Gautam Gupta , Dikshant Kukreja , Unique Kumar , Avni Mittal , Deepanshu Dabas , Kshitij Sah , Erik Cambria , Rajiv Ratn Shah , Zhengkui Wang , Avinash Anand
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07891](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07891)
- **Abstract**:
> Quantization and pruning reduce the cost of deploying language models, but their effects are usually reported as benchmark scores. Such scores record only the loss that remains at the output. Inside the model, compression damages some components more than others, and the rest of the network can partly compensate. The observed loss therefore reflects both the damage and this compensation, which benchmark scores cannot separate. How the two combine, and whether their balance changes with model size, remains unclear. We study this compensation for in-context retrieval, a capability carried by a small, identifiable set of attention heads. To separate the two, the expected loss from ablating these heads is decomposed exactly into the part restored by the rest of the network, attributed to the components responsible, and the part that reaches the output. This decomposition is then compared across model sizes. For damage of comparable size, smaller models restore most of the loss, whereas larger models retain most of it, because the compensating responses offset less of it as models grow. Compressing the heads produces the same pattern. Compression that is nearly harmless in small models can therefore be costly in large ones.

- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07870)

## 17. The Intrusion Pathway: How Non-Evidential Context Degrades Language Model Performance
- **Authors**: Dikshant Kukreja , Gautam Gupta , Avni Mittal , Avinash Anand , Kritarth Prasad , Zhengkui Wang , Timothy Liu , Rajiv Ratn Shah , Aik Beng Ng , Simon See , Erik Cambria
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07778)
- **Abstract**:
> Language models lose accuracy when a prompt contains non-evidential context: irrelevant statements from unrelated domains, or random off-distribution tokens, with no competing answer and no semantic overlap with the query. Such noise pervades retrieval-augmented, tool-using, and long-context deployments, yet the failure has lacked a mechanistic account. We trace the corruption to the Intrusion Pathway, which starts with MLPs enriching both query-subject and irrelevant-token representations, parroting heads carry prefix-token content to the prediction position, and relational readers largely avoid unrelated sources. Nevertheless, preserved subject enrichment and selective reading do not prevent intrusion in the broader computation. We then localize part of this interference to a sparse set of attention heads, which we call intrusion heads. Their prefix-derived writes promote distractor-related content, while their reduced support for the correct answer arises primarily from weaker non-prefix contributions rather than direct suppression by the prefix. The same heads transfer across tasks and distractor types and are largely weight-disjoint from the heads that integrate relevant contextual evidence, enabling a class-conditional intervention. Replacing the corrupted last-token residual with its clean counterpart, an oracle probe that requires a matched clean forward pass, recovers 97.6% on average of the distractor-induced drop across four downstream tasks. A deployable mask level knockout of about ten heads, roughly 1% of the model's attention heads, recovers 57% at bounded cost to helpful context.

## 20. Who Should Know What? Empirical Information Design for Multi-Agent LLM Economies
- **Authors**: Mete Erdogan , Mirac Suzgun , Aneesh Pappu , Guowei Xu , Batu El , Andrew A. Schoen , Furong Huang , John P. Dickerson , Mert Pilanci , James Zou

## 21. Don't Prompt Your Agents. Equip Them: Skillware as a Modular Standard for Sovereign, Auditable, and Deterministic Capability Execution for Logical Systems
- **Authors**: Vladimiros Peilivanidis (ARPA Hellenic Logical Systems)
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07652)
- **Abstract**:
> Current autonomous logical systems rely on a fragile assumption: that language models should dynamically write code, guess API payloads, and memorize operational constraints through prompt engineering. In practice, this burns massive context windows, inflates API bills, and produces brittle agents that fail unpredictably in production. In this work, we present Skillware, an open architectural framework and capability registry that formally separates what an agent wants to do from how that task is executed. Instead of prompting models with loose text instructions or markdown files baptized as 'skills', Skillware equips them with modular, versioned software capability bundles. At inference, the graph teacher is discarded; prediction requires only a single CLIP-LoRA forward pass and a cache-based classifier. Across 11 few-shot benchmarks, SD-LoRA improves over a strong CLIP-LoRA baseline by 2.4/1.8/3.0/2.4/2.0 percentage points in the 1/2/4/8/16-shot settings, respectively, without additional graph computation at test time. Results on base-to-novel generalization, cross-dataset transfer, out-of-distribution robustness, and calibration further show that training-time structural supervision improves both accuracy and transfer reliability. ## 80. Batch-Conditioned Semantic Anchors for Robust Transductive Adaptation of Vision--Language Models
- **Authors**: Mohammed Rahman Sherif Khan Mohammad , Ardhendu Behera , Sandip Pradhan , Swagat Kumar , Amr Ahmed
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06993)
- **Abstract**:
> Transductive adaptation improves vision-language models at test time by refining predictions on an unlabeled batch, but current methods face a key trade-off. Aggressive approaches exploit batch structure but can drift when batches are sparse, have prior shift, or cover little of the label space. Conservative methods better preserve CLIP text semantics, but fixed semantic references can under-adapt even when the batch provides strong corrective evidence. We propose JPTA (Joint Prior and Transport Adaptation), a prior-aware framework that casts transductive VLM adaptation as batch-conditioned semantic reference estimation. Instead of treating CLIP text prototypes as fixed priors or freely replaceable, JPTA views them as semantic references that shift toward image-side batch structure only under calibrated evidence. It estimates batch priors and soft image-side prototypes to form transported semantic references whose impact is scaled by batch reliability. Across standard benchmarks, low effective-class regimes, all-class evaluation, and online streams, JPTA consistently outperforms TransCLIP and StatA, with the largest gains on sparse and prior-shifted batches. Anchor-drift, active-support, and absent-class-mass diagnostics show that JPTA fixes effective-class mismatch without unconstrained transductive drift, indicating that robust transductive VLM adaptation should recalibrate semantic references using reliable batch evidence rather than choose between unrestricted transduction and fixed prototypes. ## 81. Interactive Backdoor Attacks in LLM-Based Proactive Dialogue Systems
- **Authors**: Zhiyang Xia , Yuming Shang , Xi Zhang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06969)
- **Abstract**:
> LLM-based proactive dialogue systems provide goal-directed support through sustained interaction. Fine-tuning their underlying models on externally supplied dialogue data introduces backdoor risks. Existing LLM backdoor studies primarily focus on eliciting an attacker-specified response upon trigger activation. We present an interactive backdoor attack framework that uses training-data poisoning to steer post-trigger, multi-turn interactions toward an attacker-specified objective. Poisoned fine-tuning dialogues implant a trigger-conditioned guidance-policy bias that ordinary user utterances can activate, without deployment-time prompt modification or further attacker intervention. Specifically, we instantiate three complementary attacks under progressively expanded post-trigger interaction settings: (1) Direct Attack, which targets an immediate response deviation upon trigger activation, retaining the conventional trigger--response setting; (2) Constrained Progressive Attack, which progressively advances the attack objective along a predefined multi-turn trajectory; and (3) Unconstrained Progressive Attack, which extends target-directed steering to open-ended interactions with varied user continuations. Using psychological counseling as a representative setting, experiments on two LLM backbones and two dialogue datasets show that all three attacks effectively achieve their objectives under their respective interaction settings while largely preserving normal utility. Separate defense evaluations show that the tested training-data filters retain nearly all poisoned dialogues, while three post-admission baselines leave substantial residual attack-specific success.

## 82. KG-AST for Cheaper CI/CD: Budgeted Test Selection and AI Bug Review
- **Authors**: Henry Yeung
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06950)
- **Abstract**:
> Continuous integration and AI-assisted review spend the same budget on every commit, although few commits introduce bugs. We study KG-AST, which scores each commit risk from git history and code-graph features before heavy tests or AI review run, so the budget goes to the riskiest commits first, and which hands the AI reviewer only a graph slice around the change. On 155 open-source projects in 11 languages, a single cross-language model trained on other projects ranks commits so that heavy testing of the riskiest 20% covers 49-64% of bug-introducing commits depending on the language, against 20% for random selection and 40-55% for testing the biggest change first. Every language beats the biggest-change baseline, by 3.8 to 13.2 points. In 20 Java projects the gain at a 20% budget is 15.1 points (64.6% vs 49.5%, 95% interval +11.6 to +18.4), and the model wins in 19 of the 20 projects. About twelve training projects suffice; richer features, tuning and cleaner labels did not help. An AI reviewer that sees only a commit's diff finds 26% of later-fixed defects exactly (32% with graph context), and about half of its findings that did not match a later fix were judged real by independent AI judges. A static call-graph selector runs about 8% of a test suite while including a test written for the fix in 85% of 67 fix commits. The Poincare embedding used in our retrieval work adds nothing measurable here: commit-risk AUC, bug review and severity ranking are unchanged or worse. The labels are noisy and the budget metric assumes a heavy test catches the bug it covers; no CI minutes were measured.
