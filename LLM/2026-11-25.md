## 1. MetaFORGE: A Meta-Learning Framework for Harness Optimization in Automated Code Generation for Parallel Computing
- **Authors**: Scott Piersall , Yang Gao , Shenyang Liu , Liqiang Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33119](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33119)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) can generate parallel programs, but their correctness and performance depend strongly on the instructions that guide generation. We present \textsc{MetaFORGE}, an execution-grounded meta-learning framework that iteratively optimizes persistent natural-language skill specifications for MPI C code generation. \textsc{MetaFORGE} combines correctness testing, compiler diagnostics, static-analysis findings, runtime measurements, and rank-level load-imbalance profiles into a scalar reward that drives textual-gradient refinement, while an external memory distills and retrieves reward-supported optimization knowledge across runs. Across six MPI tasks and three workload sizes, \textsc{MetaFORGE} improves static-analysis quality, runtime, and load balance relative to zero-shot generation and generally exceeds the evaluated \textsc{TextGrad} iterative baseline. A model-matched refinement ablation shows that the MetaFORGE advantage persists when its refinement model is reduced to \texttt{gpt-4o}. An expanded retrieval ablation across all 18 task--workload configurations shows consistent runtime benefit and broad static-analysis and load-balance benefit from retrieved context. Leakage-controlled held-out and zero-update experiments further provide evidence that optimization knowledge persists in both the transferred skill and external memory under the evaluated protocol.

## 2. Delegent: A Multi-Agent Harness That Reduces Cost without Sacrificing Performance
- **Authors**: Lingwei Gu , Jimmy Lin
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33098](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33098)
- **Abstract**:
> Agents built on frontier language models are expensive. At every step, the frontier model re-reads its entire context and executes every subtask regardless of its difficulty. This paper explores the simple thesis that much of this cost can be removed by delegating easy subtasks to a small language model, or SLM, that runs on hardware one already owns. To test it, we build Delegent, a simple and effective agent harness built on Claude Code, in which the frontier model stays in charge of every task but hands focused subtasks, such as investigating code, running tests, and searching a corpus, to SLM subagents. Existing approaches such as cascades and model routers usually choose which model executes the entire task, whereas in Delegent, the frontier model always leads the execution and delegates easy subtasks to the SLM. No router or model is trained, so either model can be swapped freely. With the help of a 27B FP8 SLM, Delegent reduces the frontier cost of long-horizon coding by 67% and of agentic search by 59%, with no loss in task performance, and even a 2B SLM that fits on a laptop reduces the coding cost by 66%. The savings grow with the legwork that the frontier model would otherwise do itself, and a short instruction, which leaves the main coding tasks to the frontier model, accounts for most of the saving and keeps the 2B SLM from losing tasks. ## 3. Auditing the Judge: Human-Derived Bias Discovery, Quantification, and Mitigation in LLM Judges
- **Authors**: Hamin Koo , Chanjoo Jung , Fangzhao Wu , Jaehyung Kim
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33065](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33065)
- **Abstract**:
> While Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as automated evaluators in evaluation and training pipelines, their judgements are often affected by systematic biases that conflict with human preferences. Although prior work has identified various biases and proposed methods for detecting and mitigating them, less attention has been paid to using human preferences to inform bias identification and mitigation. Moreover, these approaches rely heavily on pre-discovered bias lists, overlook bias strength, and depend on costly interventions. In this work, we propose HUB-J, an integrated framework based on human-derived bias factors that detects, quantifies, and mitigates biases in LLM-as-a-Judge systems. Our approach leverages human-LLM judgement disagreement cases to automatically discover interpretable bias factors, and utilizes agreement cases to quantify bias strength through controlled input modifications and resulting shifts in model decisions. Finally, building on these quantified biases, we introduce a lightweight, training-free regression-based mitigation strategy that estimates the influence of each bias factor on model judgments and corrects bias-influenced decisions by removing its estimated effect. Empirical results show that HUB-J uncovers both known and novel bias factors, reveals meaningful differences in model susceptibility, and consistently reduces bias-driven decision flips while generalizing across models.

## 4. Can LLM Judges Catch Unsafe Clinical Actions? A Fault-Injection Study
- **Authors**: Neda Ghafouri
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33012](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33012)
- **Abstract**:
> As clinical AI agents begin to interact with electronic health records (EHRs), large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to evaluate their actions. But how reliably can these judges recognize clinically dangerous mistakes, especially when an agent appears to have completed its task successfully? We investigate this question using MedAgentBench by introducing controlled clinical errors into verified agent trajectories. The experiments include 416 faulty trajectories, eight error categories, and six open-weight LLM judges. By comparing each faulty trajectory with its correct counterpart, we measure whether judges genuinely recognize errors rather than simply flagging more actions as unsafe. The findings show that access to clinical evidence significantly improves judge reliability. When shown only the final answer, judges cannot distinguish errors that leave it unchanged, while flagging 56% of correct trajectories. With full trajectory information, the ability to distinguish unsafe actions from correct ones reaches a score of 0.75, and providing actual EHR responses improves discrimination by 0.39. Surprisingly, including the agent's reasoning does not improve overall performance and reduces detection of incorrectly reported clinical values by 0.18, which remain the hardest errors to detect. MedGemma performed worse than the general-purpose model and was the only judge to miss potentially dangerous medication overdoses. We also find that the benchmark's original grader accepts all 38 trajectories with an intentionally skipped laboratory check, while combining LLM judges with a rule-based checker informed by the full EHR record detects 97% of injected faults.

## 5. LoomKV: Lossless KV Cache Compression from GPU Memory to SSD for LLM Serving
- **Authors**: Wentao Zhu , Ziao Sheng , Peng Deng , Huiqi Hu , Xuan Zhou , Xing Wei , Zhuang Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32954)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) serving engines offload the key/value (KV) cache from GPU memory to host DRAM and SSD so multi-turn and agent requests can reuse prompt prefixes. Capacity then limits service at two levels: external tiers govern prefix reuse before eviction, while the GPU KV pool bounds concurrent long requests, and lossy compression alters KV values and outputs. LoomKV brings lossless KV compression to both external tiers (tier mode) and the GPU pool (pool mode) on an unmodified engine. Using a calibrated per-head two-level 3-bit code over BF16 high bytes (sign and upper seven exponent bits), LoomKV stores BF16 KV at 0.71 of its raw size across nine models and FP8 KV at 0.85-0.87. In tier mode, TileStream writes exact-length records with length readback off the load path, leaving tier storage, eviction, and prefetch unchanged, and decompresses fetched records straight into scattered GPU pages. In pool mode, FragPack packs variable-length records into smaller shared-budget GPU pages read in place by a fused decode-attention kernel faster than FlashAttention-3 on BF16. Built with LMCache and vLLM, LoomKV makes compressed tiers act as 1.4x larger raw tiers on AgentX: across LMCache's LRU cliff, tier mode delivers 2.7-3.6x raw's goodput and 1.9-3.1x lower mean time-to-first-token (TTFT), shifts the cliff identically under other simulated replacement policies, and cuts repeated-prompt TTFT up to 1.7x via SSD-to-DRAM promotion. When tiers hold the working set, pool mode fits 1.31-1.34x as many GPU tokens, admitting 12 long requests with roughly raw's queue and goodput at 8. On an AI IDE trace where both tiers and the GPU pool are constrained, tier and pool modes raise goodput by 1.24x and 1.59x. Where raw capacity suffices, compression costs at most 2.2% throughput; tier mode is bit-exact with raw tiers, and pool mode matches FlashAttention-3's numerical accuracy.

## 6. InstructReplay: Replaying Assistant Turns to Preserve General Behavior During Factual Fine-Tuning
- **Authors**: Xiaofeng Shi , Qian Kou , Hua Zhou
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32918](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32918)
- **Abstract**:
> Fine-tuning an instruction-tuned language model on new facts often erodes its general abilities. We find that standard fine-tuning lowers instruction-following, reasoning and coding benchmarks by 35 points on average, and that these losses track a collapse in answer length: fine-tuned models at least halve their responses, and the further a response drifts from the initial model's length, the more often it fails. We propose InstructReplay: the initial model answers a set of general instructions once, and during fine-tuning the model is kept close to the initial model on these answers. Across three model families and two domains, InstructReplay reduces the average decline on five general benchmarks from 28.1 to 2.4 points and keeps answer length close to the initial model's, while learning the new facts as well as standard fine-tuning. It balances learning and retention better than seven adaptation methods in all six settings. Controlled comparisons attribute the gain to replaying the model's own answers to general instructions rather than to the form of the loss.

## 7. Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches Them to Act on What They Believe
- **Authors**: Richmond Sin Jing Xuan , Peilin Feng , Wenbin Wang , Soujanya Poria
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32884)
- **Abstract**:
> AI agents working on long tasks often commit to an answer before they have enough evidence, a behavior called laziness. In our evaluations, they frequently commit even while their stated belief still names several possible answers. An outcome reward at the end of the episode scores only the final answer, so it cannot teach the agent when to commit, because a lucky guess receives the same reward as an answer backed by evidence. During training, however, the simulator can compute the exact posterior over the answer at every step and tell whether the evidence is already enough. We propose Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB), which rewards the agent for taking further steps while this posterior still allows several answers and for committing once the evidence is sufficient. The reward needs no judge, critic or reward model, and it never reads the belief line the agent writes before each action. We also introduce Pro Worlds, a benchmark of visual puzzles where the agent views a large world through a small window. We evaluate three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of which are never seen in training. RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average. Under the outcome reward, GRPO and PPO fall below the base model on average. RLVB also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge by 9.3% on average while using 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours. The trained agent answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of the episodes on the three benchmarks, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% for the base model. Its accuracy gain therefore comes from answering more often at high levels of evidence. The belief line shows how the agent reaches this evidence: it acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and its line still names several answers, against 3 to 68% for the base model.

## 8. Measurement Artifacts in Trustworthiness Evaluations of Quantized Small Language Models
- **Authors**: Tanzeel Abbas , Tehseen Zia , David Windridge
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32848)
- **Abstract**:
> Evaluations of quantized language models attribute the change in a benchmark score to quantization. We write the measured change as the true change plus a term contributed by the instrument. Instrument error that is the same at every precision can shrink a paired comparison but cannot create or reverse an effect. Error that interacts with quantization can do both, and it is reported as an effect of quantization. We estimate this interaction for four measurement choices in 8 instruction-tuned models below two billion parameters, at float16, LLM.int8() and NF4, on identical items. Measured against labels from two human annotators, the 4-bit rise in harmful compliance is about half to two-thirds of the rise in non-refusal that a corrected key word detector records. Cloze and lettered multiple-choice scoring disagree about the effect of quantization far more than their noise allows: their estimates correlate at \r{ho} = 0.10, against a ceiling of about 0.77. LLM.int8() answers and generations depend on which items share a batch, and padding alone changes its answers. Rounding activations to 8 bits is enough to produce this dependence. NF4 is batch-invariant. The two largest reported calibration losses are response-bias shifts. We release item-level predictions, option scores and human annotations, and give recommendations for evaluating compressed small models.

## 9. CAPTA: Identifying LLMs Behind AI Agents
- **Authors**: Kailun Yan , Yue Huang , Sisi Duan
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32686](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32686)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM)-based agents access remote services through tools to complete user tasks, often via API calls. In this setting, a remote service may want to identify the backend LLM of an agent to launch model-specific attacks, including prompt injection or selective delivery of manipulated content. However, the service cannot directly query the backend model and must instead collect observable responses through its interactions with the agent, making identification challenging. In this paper, we present Completely Automated Preference Test to tell LLMs Apart (CAPTA), a model identification approach inspired by CAPTCHA (Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart), which distinguishes humans from automated programs. CAPTA presents multiple-choice questions as verification challenges, using differences in models' selection preferences to identify the backend LLM. It selects questions based on cross-model option-generation diversity and uses LLM-guided search to optimize their option sets. Our evaluation shows that CAPTA achieves 99.0% mean identification accuracy with only 10 questions across 25 LLMs from 10 model families. We further evaluate CAPTA with four widely used agents and seven representative LLMs, achieving 99.7% mean identification accuracy across all 28 agent-LLM combinations with 30 submitted answers.

## 10. Economic World Model

We then propose a general framework for studying EWMs that links three tasks: simulating agent behaviour, learning economic transitions and systematically evaluating the dynamics. Economists can contribute richer economic models and knowledge, while AI researchers can develop stronger data-driven methods for simulating agent behaviour and learning economic transitions. ## 20. How Much Dependency Can a Marginal Rule See? Total Correlation in Diffusion LM Parallel Decoding
- **Authors**: Dovud Asadov , Rustambek Urokov , Qobiljon Toshnazarov , Abduaziz Kayumov , Mardon Hazratov , Husanboy Mansuraliyev
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31990)
- **Abstract**:
> A masked diffusion language model is fast because it commits several tokens per step. Each token is sampled from its own marginal, so the dependency between the tokens is lost, and the size of that loss is the conditional total correlation C of the committed set. Most decoders choose the set from per-position marginals, which can bound C but cannot determine it. We ask how well they predict it. We estimate C for fixed candidate sets on four masked diffusion LMs from two families. ## 120. The Date You Didn't Set: The System-Prompt Date as a Hidden Variable in LLM Evaluation
- **Authors**: Noureldin Zahran , Mohammed E. Fouda , Khaled N. Khasawneh
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31254)
- **Abstract**:
> The system prompt of many instruction-tuned models carries a date, usually today's, filled in automatically for conversational and agentic requests alike, so an unchanged evaluation can send a different prompt each day. In this paper, we explore how much this hidden input changes evaluation outcomes. Across six instruction-tuned models and four benchmarks, with prompts, decoding, and configuration fixed, every model is affected: two plausible dates (2024--2026) switch a median $6.6\%$ of test items between correct and incorrect. Even rerunning the evaluation one day later flips about as many items as changing the batch size, although nothing in the setup was changed. Yet none of the eleven evaluation stacks we audit pins the date. With the date and the rest of the setup fixed, reruns reproduce byte-for-byte; we therefore recommend pinning, logging, and reporting the date.

## 121. Validation Bias in Large Language Model Responses to Cancer Treatment Queries: A Factorial Cross-Sectional Study
- **Authors**: Ramez Kouzy , Saketh Vinjamuri , Sarah Ghalayini , Jack Gallifant , Shan Chen , Marielle Fis Loperana , Roxanna Attar-Olyaee , Danielle S. Bitterman
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31251)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) chatbots are increasingly used by patients seeking cancer information, but their safety when users present evidence-misaligned beliefs about treatment remains uncharacterized. We hypothesized that LLMs exhibit validation bias, affirming rather than correcting such beliefs, and evaluated this with a 2x2x2 factorial design crossing conversation format (single-turn vs multi-turn), treatment type (evidence-based vs alternative), and query valence (positive vs negative). Framing was evidence-aligned (supporting evidence-based treatments or questioning alternative therapies) or evidence-misaligned (promoting alternative treatments or questioning evidence-based therapies). We analyzed 1,272 conversations (6,360 interactions) across 14 LLMs from 6 vendors, scored by an LLM judge (GPT-5-mini) validated against human annotation on a 6-dimension safety rubric. LLMs exhibited significant validation bias in oncology, with unsafe responses nearly tripling under evidence-misaligned framing. Evaluations limited to neutral, fact-seeking queries may overestimate real-world safety, and safety testing of medical AI systems should include evidence-misaligned user framing.

## 122. When Can a Rewiring Control Test LLM Weight Readers? C2 and an Eligibility Protocol
- **Authors**: Vaishak Girish Kumar , Sanika Vilas Najan
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29120)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models can predict the outputs of small neural networks from raw weights, but success could reflect recognition of weight-space regularities (fingerprints) rather than computation, and function-preserving controls cannot tell these apart. We propose C2, a counterfactual that permutes the columns of a network's final linear readout: every other parameter, and the readout's singular values, norms and entry multiset, are unchanged, and the function can change in an exactly recomputable way; we verify that each evaluated permutation does. C2 separates fingerprinting from computation only under three conditions, which we state as an eligibility protocol: a strategy restricted to C2-invariant summaries must already predict the target; C2 must change a property specific to the rewiring; and the reader's identity must be attested. No natural setting we tested is eligible. On tiny MLPs a frontier reader route followed the rewired function on all 24 networks, but invariant baselines were near chance and its backend could not be attested. In two public CNN zoos the conditions traded off: weight statistics predicted CIFAR-10 accuracy in-distribution, but C2's effect on accuracy was not specific to the swap, while per-image targets were specific but not predictable from invariant summaries. On a constructed population where a planted fingerprint rule works, all conditions hold and C2 separates a rule-following reader from an executor. A self-hosted Qwen3.8-27B followed the rewired function rather than the shortcut, and showed no evidence of using the weights with thinking disabled. A registered replication on fresh networks confirmed both findings (tracking minus stuck 0.875, 95% interval [0.750, 1.000]), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B also followed the rewiring (tracking minus stuck 0.729 [0.552, 0.885]).
