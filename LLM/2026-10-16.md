## 1. MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering
- **Authors**: Shangheng Du , Jinxin Shi , Shuaiyu Zhang , Yiming Wu , Junpeng Zhao , Shiyang Feng , Xin Li , Liang He , Xiangchao Yan , Bo Zhang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22344](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22344)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) are expanding from assisting scientific research and engineering to autonomously developing and improving AI systems, advancing AI for AI (AI4AI) as a pathway toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). Workflow-driven machine learning engineering (MLE) systems provide a practical path toward this pursuit through complex orchestration that manages long-horizon experimentation, while recent MLE systems adopt model-directed interaction, allowing models to autonomously organize experiments and adapt optimization strategies based on execution feedback. However, feedback from each experiment reflects several preceding decisions, and a long MLE run contains many such costly experiments. Training must therefore select useful supervision from long interaction trajectories and learn from intermediate outcomes. We propose a post-training method for autonomous long-horizon MLE optimization and apply it to train MLExpert, a 35B Mixture-of-Experts agentic model. We design the Kaggle-MLE Factory to construct executable tasks and an interaction harness for model-directed experimentation. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and coding capabilities to MLE through outcome-guided supervision. Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL) treats the multi-turn decisions between successive experiment results as an execution window and assigns it a shared outcome-based reward. Asynchronous collection and policy correction support policy updates before the trajectory ends. On MLE-Bench Lite, MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to 72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger frontier models such as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. Further experiments show that MLExpert improves performance on NatureBench Lite, agent scaffold optimization, and algorithm optimization, suggesting that the learned optimization capability generalizes to other tasks.

## 2. Evaluation Awareness Beyond English: Do LLMs Act Differently When Tested in Indic Languages?
- **Authors**: Trizal Garg , Sibayan Mitra , Arya Karanjkar , Advait Singh , Jagat Sesh Challa , Yash Sinha , Siddharth Mehrotra , Dhruv Kumar , Pratham Yogish Maliya
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22333](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22333)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) can often tell when they are being tested. This ability, known as evaluation awareness, can lead models to act more cautiously during safety evaluations than in real use. Existing work has studied evaluation awareness almost entirely in English, although models serve users in many languages. Indic languages are spoken by over a billion people, yet evaluation awareness remains almost entirely unstudied in them. We test four instruction-tuned models from three families in five Indic languages alongside English. A set of cues varies whether the model is told that it is monitored and whether the monitoring is a safety test. Models refuse harmful requests more often when they are told that they are being tested. This increase appears in all four models and all six languages. In Indic languages, refusal of harmful requests rises by 14.4 percentage points on average. The test cue also brings Indic refusal of harmful requests level with English, but raises refusal of benign Indic requests from 6.9% to 11.3%. We release all 47,880 judged responses to support further work. These results suggest that safety evaluations in Indic languages may not reflect how models treat their everyday users.

## 3. Designing Transformers for Continuous Diffusion Language Models
- **Authors**: Weijian Luo , Yujian Chen , Hao Jiang , Guang Cheng , Tianyang Hu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22330)
- **Abstract**:
> Continuous diffusion language models (CDLMs) offer an alternative approach to parallel language generation; however, most existing studies directly use transformer architectures from either causal large language models (LLMs) or Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) for image generation, which were not optimized for CDLMs. In this paper, we conduct a systematic study on Transformer architectures for CDLMs and introduce Language Diffusion Transformer (LangDiT), combining feature and query-key normalization, wider noise conditioning, likelihood-shortcut gradient routing and calibration, and confidence-gated training self-conditioning. LangDiT significantly reduces generation perplexity in matched LangFlow comparisons, establishing a new state of the art in continuous language generation across different model formulations. Using LangFlow as a representative CDLM, LangDiT as a plug-and-play backbone leads to a new state-of-the-art model with a generation perplexity (GenPPL) of 21.72 using 1024 NFEs and 44.72 GenPPL using 128 NFEs on the unconditional generation of 1024-token sequences on the OpenWebText dataset. When adapted to Plaid-style CDLM formulations, LangDiT results in a new leading GenPPL of 11.33 using 1024 denoising NFEs, and 27.51 GenPPL using 128 denoising NFEs. These gains are achieved while retaining each model's underlying representation, training objective, and inference sampler, isolating transformer architecture as a fundamental source of improvement. Our study identifies architectural design as an important source of improvement in continuous diffusion language models.

## 4. Private Retrieval Augmented Generation via Random Projection
- **Authors**: Dixi Yao , Tahseen Rabbani , Tian Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22297)
- **Abstract**:
> Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enhances the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by querying external structured knowledge. However, it can also introduce privacy risks by leaking sensitive information from the retrieval datastore. We propose a simple method to preserve datastore privacy in RAG systems via random projection. By applying the same projection to both datastore embeddings and query embeddings, our method provably preserves semantic similarity between queries and retrieved items while substantially mitigating data extraction attacks. Across multiple RAG architectures and datasets, we show that this lightweight approach achieves superior retrieval and generation performance compared to prior methods which leverage formal differential privacy (DP) guarantees, while exhibiting comparable empirical privacy under strong attack models. Our results for the first time suggest that random projection can serve as a competitive and practical baseline for privacy-preserving RAG systems.

## 5. Improving Environment-Agnostic Coding Agents: The Role of Scaffolding
- **Authors**: Sergey Rodionov
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22289)
- **Abstract**:
> Stronger language models may increasingly construct useful procedures for themselves, reducing the advantage of developer-supplied scaffolding. We investigate why this advantage persists and whether an environment-agnostic agent can be improved without environment-specific engineering. We evaluate a Codex-based agent in two nearly identical prompt variants on ARC-AGI-3, AGI Maze, and Terminal-Bench. Each variant is deployed with unchanged code, prompts, and general-purpose tools across environments, receiving environment-specific information as input. Changing only an instruction to encourage reporting difficulties and returning control for automatic continuation improves mean scores on both ARC-AGI-3 and AGI Maze, while Terminal-Bench shows small, mixed changes. Yet the general agent remains below an ARC-AGI-3-specific reference, including with GPT-5.6-sol. The reference prescribes organization and control rather than supplying new game rules or solutions. To investigate this gap, we transfer reset decisions to a scripted controller that follows a convention already given to the agent. This small change recovers part of the specialized agent's advantage. A complementary AGI Maze experiment shows that additional tutorial experience can reduce performance. Together, these findings highlight the distinction between having information and tools available and using them effectively. Supplied procedures still matter for the models tested here. The performance gains also come with higher token-based cost. The experiments do not separate better use of information and procedures from additional or differently allocated inference. We provide the EnvTraverse framework and an evaluated baseline agent for testing whether further environment-independent changes improve performance in some environments without degrading it in others.

## 7. Disagreement-Guided Weak-to-Strong Routing
- **Authors**: Sima Noorani , Shayan Kiyani , George Pappas , Hamed Hassani
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22245)
- **Abstract**:
> Small language models are increasingly deployed on edge devices and robotic systems to meet cost and latency constraints, yet their limited capabilities can make reliable deployment challenging. Stronger models can provide assistance when needed, but deciding when to route a weak-model response to a stronger model often relies on signals of response correctness. In open-ended and agentic tasks, however, obtaining such signals can require costly ground-truth supervision that is difficult to obtain at scale. We revisit the foundations of weak-to-strong routing and ask whether routing decisions can instead be guided by signals that do not require ground-truth supervision. We first characterize the optimal weak-to-strong routing policy, while allowing for multiple weak-model responses generated through local inference. Building on this characterization, we derive a principled approximation to the optimal routing based on model-to-model comparison. In particular, we introduce a notion of model disagreement that can be measured directly from model-generated responses, without ground-truth supervision. Importantly, the approximation becomes increasingly accurate as the strong model becomes a better proxy for the ground-truth distribution. Across agentic and single-turn tasks, our approach effectively navigates the trade-off between routing cost and response accuracy without ground-truth supervision. Moreover, even when ground-truth correctness signals are available for training, disagreement-based routing can outperform methods trained directly to predict correctness

## 8. AutoSFT: An Agentic System for Automated Data Research in Supervised Fine-Tuning
- **Authors**: Bingxiang He , Pengqi Sun , Shihao Peng , Shaoxuan Li , Huiyu Xiao , Hongjian Shi , Zixuan Fu , Ziqing Qiao , Yuxin Zuo , Cheng Qian , Xinqiang Yu , Jiayi Li , Xiusi Chen , Yizhen Song , Xiangyang Wang , Iofai Lei , Yudong Wang , Ning Ding , Zhiyuan Liu , Xiaozhi Wang , Chaojun Xiao
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22194](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22194)
- **Abstract**:
> Language model agents increasingly automate post-training, but when given the full training stack they converge on supervised fine-tuning and spend most effort revising training data. Existing systems give the agent one kind of control, selecting rows from a fixed pool or calling a generator, whereas a human data engineer adjusts proportions, prunes, repairs, replaces and synthesizes the data. We design a data action space for these changes and ask whether an automated system can make the full range rather than one of them, and what structure that requires. We first run this loop with a single agent, and it reaches only one action, adjusting category proportions, while its context fills with execution output and is discarded mid-search. We therefore introduce \textbf{AutoSFT}, a multi-agent system where a meta agent separates execution from analysis, gathers proposals from independent angles, and calls a synthesis subagent for capabilities the pool cannot cover, with the model, the trainer, and the budget fixed within each comparison. Adding each component in turn helps: the executor halves context loss, the analysis subagents lift the best IFEval by $+6.5$, and synthesis adds $+3.4$ IFEval and $+2.9$ IFBench. The same loop transfers to code, where analysis adds $+3.7$ MBPP+ over a single agent that does not improve. Every result comes from runs that retrain from the base model each round, so each component's effect is individually attributable. Our findings suggest that automating SFT data research depends more on covering diverse data actions than on a stronger single action.

## 10. RAG-RST: Retrieved Exemplars as In-Context Discourse Schema for RST Parsing
- **Authors**: Mohammadreza Sediqin , Shlomo Engelson Argamon
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22150)
- **Abstract**:
> In Rhetorical Structure Theory (RST), each edge of a discourse tree carries a nuclearity label, marking which span holds the central content, and a relation drawn from a closed inventory of functional categories. Neither label is a surface property: the same two clauses can be Elaboration or Background depending on rhetorical intent, and the boundary between them is fixed by annotation convention rather than by lexical cues. Predicting these labels therefore requires a formal discourse schema, and exposure to discourse-rich pretraining text does not supply it: given only a target EDU pair, a strong LLM recovers nuclearity at 0.794 F1 and relation at 0.738 F1. We propose RAG-RST, a retrieval-augmented framework that supplies this schema as in-context demonstration rather than through parameter updates, raising nuclearity to 0.846 F1 and relation to 0.788 F1 with no training. Two configurations are compared: a precomputed strategy that labels all EDU pairs before parsing, and a dynamic strategy that retrieves at each reduce step, so that the exemplars reaching the generator depend on the reductions the parser has already committed to. Both outperform existing RST parsers across span, nuclearity, relation, and full tree metrics, with the dynamic variant consistently strongest. Two properties of the retrieved schema emerge: its utility is bounded in number, saturating and then declining beyond fifty exemplars, and it depends on structural position. Both indicate that a formal linguistic schema can be supplied to a foundation model as a demonstration rather than trained into its parameters.

## 11. Are You Sure Your LLM Is Sure? Repairing and Auditing Confidence
- **Authors**: Arijit Dey , Bhartendu Kumar , Ramya Hebbalaguppe , Khuushi Maheshwari
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22139)
- **Abstract**:
> Confidence scores determine whether an LLM answer is accepted, reviewed, or regenerated, yet scalar recalibration can only correct errors visible through the score itself. We introduce RADAR (Repair, Adopt, and Diagnose After Recalibration), a reliability framework that separates confidence repair from deciding whether to deploy the repair and auditing the errors that remain. Under squared loss, we decompose prediction risk into outcome noise, information hidden by the score, and mapping error; restricting repair to order-preserving maps additionally introduces a pooling gap. For repair, we propose Probe-Anchored Isotonic Calibration (PAIC), which learns a monotone map from correctness-probe predictions on unlabeled target prompts. PAIC's excess risk depends on the probe's score-conditional bias and fitting error rather than its overall accuracy. For adoption, we derive a paired procedure with finite-sample control of false improvements. For auditing, we develop a prompt-clustered kernel test that detects feature-aligned residual errors while accounting for dependent answers from the same prompt. Across multiple LLM checkpoints, benchmarks, and distribution shifts, richer predictors can improve confidence estimation, but projecting their information back to a scalar score can lose these gains, and lower prediction risk need not yield better review decisions. Across 47,400 replayed raw-score allocations, paired betting adopts candidates with lower held-out test risk in 42.95% of allocations, versus 15.09% for one-sided empirical Bernstein. RADAR thus provides a principled workflow to repair what a scalar score can express, adopt a repair only when supported by evidence, and diagnose systematic errors that recalibration leaves behind. Code and data will be released upon acceptance.

## 12. Believed but Not Said: Measuring Hidden Belief Collapse in Multi-Agent LLM Discussions
- **Authors**: Liangjie Huang , Henry Peng Zou , Wei-Chieh Huang , Wooseong Yang , Yuwei Han , Philip S. Yu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22138](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22138)
- **Abstract**:
> LLM systems increasingly rely on groups of agents that discuss, critique, and check one another, assuming that peers will catch individual errors. We test this assumption against a minimal adversary. In a ten-agent factual discussion, one or three adversarial agents repeatedly assert a plausible false answer. After each round we probe each benign agent's private answer through a separate call whose response is hidden from its peers, and we separately label its public stance toward the claim. Across six models and three factual-QA datasets, groups remain stable without an attacker: only 0.6% of benign agents finish with the false answer. A single adversarial agent increases this rate to 44.8% and three to 60.5%. Studies of conformity typically report public agreement without private change; we find the reverse. With three adversarial agents present, benign agents hold the false answer in 51.6% of exposed turns but publicly endorse it in only 19.7%. Much of the influence therefore remains invisible in the discussion, and the false answers persist when agents are asked for their own views. Agents that retain the true answer often fail to defend it; once adopted, the false answer spreads among benign agents, and baseline knowledge does not predict robustness.

## 13. CART: Compatible Routing for Multimodal Continual Instruction Tuning
- **Authors**: Ling-Xiang Zhang , Tao Hu , Da-Wei Zhou
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22137)
- **Abstract**:
> Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) achieve strong performance through instruction tuning, yet real-world applications often require them to continually acquire new capabilities from sequential tasks, i.e., Multimodal Continual Instruction Tuning (MCIT). In typical MLLMs, a projector is employed to align visual representations with the language model. However, existing methods primarily protect the language model from forgetting while continuously updating the projector across tasks. Such asymmetric adaptation can cause substantial representation drift in the projector, increasing the mismatch between the projector and language model and consequently exacerbating catastrophic forgetting. To address this issue, we propose Compatible Routing (CART), which explicitly coordinates the adaptation of the projector and language model. Specifically, CART maintains task-specific experts for both modules and combines them through weighted mixtures. CART jointly infers their weights by considering both expert relevance and cross-module compatibility, thereby promoting coordinated adaptation between the two modules. To further control model growth, we introduce relevance-guided expert consolidation, which merges experts exhibiting similar relevance profiles across samples within each module. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CART achieves state-of-the-art performance.

## 14. DropKV: Decoupling Residual-Output Perturbation for Near-Optimal KV-Cache Eviction
- **Authors**: Aozhong Zhang , Selcuk Gurses , Yanxia Deng , Naigang Wang , Chi-Chun Liu , Davis Wertheimer , Derrick Liu , Xin Li , Zi Yang , Felix X.-F. Ye , Penghang Yin
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22130)
- **Abstract**:
> Key-value (KV) cache management has become a critical bottleneck for scaling Large Language Models (LLMs) to long-context scenarios due to linear memory growth. While recent eviction methods have moved beyond simple attention-score heuristics to incorporate value representations, they typically face a fundamental trade-off: the underlying optimization for minimizing output perturbation is an NP-hard combinatorial problem, leading to mathematically suboptimal heuristics or implementations that are difficult to kernelize. In this work, we propose DropKV, a simple principled eviction framework based on Decoupled Residual-Output Perturbation. By decoupling the joint eviction decision into independent per-token scoring, DropKV circumvents combinatorial intractability and admits a provable constant-factor approximation guarantee under a cone condition that we empirically verify on three long-context LLMs. To ensure practical viability, we provide a high-performance implementation using fused Triton kernels that avoid materializing the full attention matrix, delivering a 19.8x scoring-kernel speedup that translates to up to a 9.9% matched-batch end-to-end prefill speedup. Extensive evaluations on the RULER and LongBench benchmarks demonstrate that DropKV consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines at equivalent cache budgets, achieving the lowest inference latency among eviction methods while also remaining faster than the dynamic cache baseline.

## 15. PROBE: Learning to Audit Policy Compliance in Tool-Using LLM Agents
- **Authors**: Kshitij Mishra , Abhijith Sharma , Nils Lukas , Salem Lahlou
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22124](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22124)
- **Abstract**:
> Modern agentic systems extend language models with external tools, enabling them to complete complex tasks through multi-turn interactions. As these agents are deployed in consequential settings, they are often constrained by policies governing pricing, privacy, confirmation, safety, and other requirements. However, existing evaluation methods leave a critical gap: capability benchmarks measure what agents can accomplish, while content-safety red-teaming measures harmful generations, but neither tests whether agents comply with predefined policies during otherwise valid tool use. Such policy violations are difficult to audit because their effects may emerge only after several turns, span multiple tool calls, or surface in downstream systems. We propose PROBE (Policy Rule Observation via Behavioral Exploration), an algorithm for training LLM-based auditors that interact with deployed agents as realistic users and probe them for policy violations through multi-turn, tool-mediated dialogue. We formalize agent auditing as a two-player Markov game and propose a composite reward that balances violation discovery, behavioral plausibility, conversation progress, and policy diversity. To study training objectives and reward design, we compare GRPO, DPO, and GFlowNet-based optimization in a controlled setting. Across extensive experiments, auditors trained with PROBE uncover substantially broader and higher-yield policy violations than same-architecture zero-shot adversaries, even when the auditor is significantly smaller than the target agent. Our results show that multi-turn behavioral auditing is a distinct and necessary axis of agent evaluation, complementing capability and safety benchmarks. PROBE offers a scalable path toward automated policy auditing for real-world agentic systems and suggests a co-evolutionary loop in which auditors expose failures and agents improve from auditor feedback.

## 16. Learning from Unverified Peers via Shared Residual Distillation
- **Authors**: Feiyang Chen , Runze Ma , Zichen Wang , Jiong Lou , Zihan Wang , Shuangjia Zheng , Jie Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22080](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22080)
- **Abstract**:
> A language model can generate several solutions to the same problem, some leading to correct answers and others to incorrect ones. Many post-training methods turn these trajectories into learning signals using supervision from reference answers, verifiable feedback, or external teachers. Without such supervision, learning from the model's own generations becomes less straightforward, as their correctness cannot be reliably assessed. An incorrect solution may nevertheless contain useful intermediate reasoning, so its value as supervision need not be determined solely by its final answer. Can a model improve by learning from its unverified peer solutions without first determining which are correct? We propose Shared Residual Distillation (SRD), an on-policy self-distillation method that learns from the model's own unverified peer trajectories. SRD compares how peers change the prediction distribution relative to a control, retaining adjustments that agree in direction and are conservative in magnitude. This yields supervision without treating any complete solution as correct, and the trained model requires no peers at inference time. Starting from the corresponding base models, SRD raises mean AIME24--26 avg@12 from 11.02 to 16.20 on Qwen3-1.7B, from 19.07 to 45.46 on Qwen3-4B, and from 21.39 to 54.44 on Qwen3-8B. Controlled ablations support the value of the shared-residual construction, and we observe no degradation relative to the base models on two general-capability benchmarks.

## 18. Prism-1: Position- and Rival-Independent Scoring for System One Models
- **Authors**: Yao Long Teng , Jianguo Huang , Yanchen Deng , Jiayi Cai , Bo An
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066)
- **Abstract**:
> Given a state and a set of options, such as intents, tools or interface elements, System One decision models return a distribution over the options in one forward pass, without generated reasoning. Web systems increasingly build them by reading decisions from language models. Two axioms of rational choice are natural requirements for such decisions, because in web systems the listing and the option set are determined by other components and can change or be manipulated: the order in which the options are listed should not matter, and adding an option should not change the relative odds of the others (Luce's choice axiom). We test the standard reads, namely single-choice question (SCQ) prompting, with shuffled-order training, order averaging or sorted options, and invariant set architectures, and find that each violates at least one axiom: one shuffle changes 12-20% of SCQ answers, sorting only freezes an arbitrary convention, and a well-chosen added option steers 4-34% of decisions. By Luce's theorem, a read satisfies both axioms only if it is a softmax over scores that each depend on one option alone, so in a transformer they hold by construction only if no token that scores an option sees another. We implement this as Prism-1 (Position- and Rival-Independent Scoring for System One Models): every option is read in its own block at shared positions, with the model's usual left-to-right attention, in one forward pass. On five benchmark sets with Qwen3-8B and Gemma 4 12B, SCQ prompting trained on the same data still changes 7% of its answers under one shuffle, and an added irrelevant option steers 12% of its decisions. Prism-1 stays within one point of that baseline's accuracy and is 2-4 points more accurate once only answers that survive a reordering are counted, while no shuffle changes any of its answers and no added irrelevant option moves its choice among the others.

## 20. GraphACM: Graph-based Action Consistency Measurement for Uncertainty Quantification in LLM Agents
- **Authors**: Yaxin Ma , Wenbo Zhang , Satya Krishna Pothapragada , Alina Zare
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22031)
- **Abstract**:
> Behavioral consistency across repeated executions provides an informative signal for assessing the reliability of large language model (LLM) agents. However, existing approaches typically summarize consistency with a single task-level score, without identifying where behavioral differences occur or whether actions are compared under equivalent decision contexts.
