## 1. Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins
- **Authors**: El Mustapha Mansouri , Eikichi Kimura , Masato Koga , Keigo Arai
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40589)
- **Abstract**:
> An autonomous research laboratory could conduct an entire study, from formulating questions to preparing manuscripts. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers one route towards this goal. Recent AI systems take on several research roles, but may draw conclusions that the evidence does not support. Here, we present an autonomous research laboratory in which agents based on large language models assess one another's work and can stop or redirect investigations. They can generate and reject research questions, require separate theoretical checks and conduct experiments designed to challenge their claims. We demonstrate these capabilities by investigating ensembles of quantum spins in diamond. The agents derived the minimum number of calibration settings needed to predict responses to a defined family of microwave pulse sequences. They checked the result computationally and designed experiments to distinguish competing predictions. An initial experiment agreed with the theoretical predictions, but repeated reference measurements showed that the signal was drifting during data collection. The agents therefore treated the result as inconclusive and revised the measurement protocol without relaxing the acceptance criteria. The revised experiment met those criteria and supported the theoretical predictions. This study demonstrates that collaborating AI agents can select research questions, derive theoretical results and test their predictions experimentally.

## 2. VeriCUA: Verification as Agentic Exploration for Computer-Using Agents
- **Authors**: Qiushi Sun , Da Yu , Alexander Knop , Amer Sinha , Lingpeng Kong , Ben Kao , Ravi Kumar , Pritish Kamath
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40578)
- **Abstract**:
> Building and scaling computer-using agents (CUAs) across desktop, mobile, and web environments requires reliable task-completion verification for data curation, evaluation, and reinforcement learning. Typical automated approaches prompt vision-language models (VLMs) to judge trajectories or generate programs to inspect application state. However, both rely on partial views of complex digital environments. VLM judges are restricted to supplied observations, while generated programs depend on accessible state. These views can omit decisive evidence or leave completion judgments unsupported. Tailoring separate verifiers to different platforms and access settings adds further engineering cost. We present VeriCUA, a platform-agnostic framework for verification through agentic exploration. It treats verification as dynamic evidence gathering and assessment within a shared procedure for online and offline settings across platforms and observation modalities. After inspecting the available context to construct a task rubric, the verifier selects GUI or code checks for unresolved requirements and combines their findings under explicit evidence rules. Experiments show improved agreement with reference labels over the compared baselines across these settings, with the largest offline gains on hard cases and stronger rejection of false completion claims online. Further analyses connect these gains to complementary evidence and test-time exploration.

## 5. On the Fundamental Limits of Prefill-Based Decoding Length Prediction
- **Authors**: Haowen Shi , Xuzhe Yan , Fahao Chen , Peng Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40541](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40541)
- **Abstract**:
> Predicting how many tokens a large language model (LLM) will decode, using only prefill-phase hidden states, is a key primitive for inference scheduling. We show that current benchmarks conflate predictor quality with task hardness: when the target model, dataset, or sampling configuration changes, reported accuracy collapses even though the predictor is unchanged. We trace this instability to an overlooked error source---the inherent stochasticity of autoregressive decoding---and derive bounds that every prefill-side method must obey. Our analysis treats decoding as a stopped stochastic process. The per-step stopping probability of a response is not constant but ramps up over hundreds of steps, so the decode length inherits a central limit theorem from the many local decisions that determine where a response ends; this yields per-prompt normality with an explicit error term, together with an affine variance law $\sigma=\alpha\mu+b$ whose slope is the coefficient of variation of the number of response segments. Empirically, the normal law reproduces the per-prompt MAE and the central mass of the decode-length distribution to within a few percent, and the residual departures from normality (mild right skew, slight excess kurtosis) are the ones predicted by the sampling-noise correction. Under fixed model, data, and sampling conditions, a broad design space of predictors (MLP, EGTP, FiLM, soft-label, and Mixture-of-Experts variants) converges to similar performance close to the bound, confirming that \emph{system error dominates model error}. We argue for a benchmarking protocol that fixes the model, data, and sampling parameters and that reports relative-error metrics alongside absolute ones. ## 8. Retrieving from Your Own Mistakes: On-Policy Training of Embedding Models for Multi-Hop Retrieval-Augmented Generation
- **Authors**: Chenjie Qiu , Zixuan Zeng , Shaobin Shi , Bingfeng Chen , Dongsheng Luo , Boyan Xu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40409)
- **Abstract**:
> Complex questions in retrieval-augmented generation require evidence spread over several documents, and an answer is reliable only when the retriever finds all of it. Chain embedding retrievers collect such evidence step by step, re-encoding the question together with the passages retrieved so far, and we find that they are already strong: trained only on gold histories, a small chain embedding retriever that calls no language model outperforms graph-based systems and is competitive with a search agent. This training, however, leaves a gap. At test time the retriever conditions on its own retrievals, which soon contain mistakes: by the third step, about half or more of the states it visits hold a wrong passage, and these passages lower its chance of retrieving a missing gold passage well beyond what the difficulty of the states explains. We propose on-policy state training, which trains the encoder on the states it reaches itself. At every epoch the current encoder retrieves chains for the training questions; each state it visits, including those built on its own mistakes, is labeled with the earliest gold passage still missing while the other missing gold passages are masked, so that no correct continuation is penalized; and the encoder is updated contrastively on these states mixed with teacher-forced ones, so that its training distribution follows it as it learns. The resulting retriever retrieves complete evidence more often than teacher-forced training and the systems we compare, and it transfers without further training to harder long-chain variants of HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA that we construct. On the same states, it recovers from its own mistakes far more often, and controlled comparisons attribute the gain to training on the states the encoder currently reaches.

## 10. Neighborhood Elites: Quality-Diversity Search for Uncertainty Quantification in Question Answering
- **Authors**: Mesut Ceylan , Arda Baris Basaran , Elgar Fleisch , Filipe Barata
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40278)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable, yet they produce wrong answers indistinguishable from correct ones. In question answering (QA), such an error propagates unnoticed into downstream tasks. Perturbation-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) addresses this by probing a model over a set of input rewrites and reading uncertainty from how much its predictions vary. Yet this set is usually built generically, by free paraphrasing, token-level perturbation, or adversarial search. Such rewrites leave two things uncontrolled that matter in QA: what kind of change is made to the context, and how explicitly the context supports the answer. When neither is controlled, the composition of the set is determined by the generator rather than by design, and the estimate inherits whatever that generator happens to produce. Quality-diversity (QD) search is built to cover a predefined exploration space, so we recast input perturbation as a QD problem and propose \emph{neighborhood elites (NE)}, a neighborhood constructor that spreads answer-preserving rewrites across two axes, the kind of edit and the explicitness of support. We compare NE against three other neighborhood constructors, holding the editor, QA model, uncertainty measure and search objective fixed, and against three scores read from the original context. Across five extractive-QA datasets and five instruction-tuned LLMs, NE improves failure detection against every other constructor in at least 24 of the 25 dataset-model pairs, reaching 0.726 AUROC and 0.211 AUPRC lift over the base rate. Additionally, NE detects failure better than semantic entropy computed over decoder samples, by 0.074 AUROC and 0.062 AUPRC lift, and this advantage widens as the neighborhood grows.

## 12. EpiAgent: Epistemic Self-Play for LLM Agents
- **Authors**: Xuanfa Jin , Xinyu Cui , Zhijian Ma , Xue Yan , Jian Zhao , Haifeng Zhang , Jun Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40227](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40227)
- **Abstract**:
> Epistemic game theory explains an agent's strategic behavior via the beliefs it holds about others and how these beliefs are updated as the interactions unfold. To bridge this gap, we propose EpiAgent, a framework that equips LLM-based agents with an explicit, learnable epistemic layer, which maintains beliefs over others' private information and combines these beliefs with the agent's available information into an epistemic state for action selection. Further analyses reveal the benefits of dedicated epistemic training, improvements in belief inference accuracy, and the contribution of learned beliefs to action planning. These results demonstrate the value of an explicit, learnable epistemic layer for effective multi-agent interactions.

## 14. Locating and Closing Information Gaps in Multi-Agent Systems
- **Authors**: Yaxin Gong , Chongming Gao , Gangyi Zhang , Chenxiao Fan , Leyang Shen , Jiakai Wang , Dong Wang , Yang Liu , Haoyan Liu , Xinting Hu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40186)
- **Abstract**:
> Multi-step Web tasks often require several LLM agents to gather and combine evidence. The communication graph of a fixed workflow restricts which agents can read each other's outputs: evidence retrieved by one agent may never reach the agent that needs it, leaving an information gap and an incomplete or incorrect answer. Adding a connection can deliver the missing evidence, but can also lead the receiver to adopt an incorrect upstream conclusion that displaces a correct answer; the final output alone does not separate the two. We propose Selective Probing and Link Insertion for Closing information gaps in multi-agent Execution (SPLICE), which locates information gaps per query and decides what to adopt after adding an edge. A selector trained on these process features chooses between adopting the candidate answer and retaining the original. On five benchmarks, SPLICE raises the task score on Web tool use by 8.0% and the mean score on four reasoning and planning benchmarks by 5.7% relative to the static skeleton, at 1.25 to 1.44$\times$ the skeleton's cost, and transfers zero-shot to four unseen backbones.

## 19. Revisiting Over-refusal in Safety-aligned Large Language Models: A Representation and Optimization Perspective
- **Authors**: Chao Chen , Pengyang Shao , Wei Qin , Shijie Hao , Fei Shen , Richang Hong
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39802)
- **Abstract**:
> Over-refusal is a critical side effect of safety alignment in large language models (LLMs), causing harmless but superficially risky queries to be mistakenly rejected. Despite the effectiveness of existing mitigation methods, why safety-aligned LLMs may still reject harmless queries under correct safety supervision remains insufficiently understood. To address this gap, we analyze over-refusal through the lens of representation and optimization. We make two observations: 1) the representations of harmful and over-refusal samples are visually distinguishable at certain intermediate layers but increasingly overlap in deeper layers; and 2) harmful-sample gradients exhibit high intra-class similarity and concentrate in a low-rank subspace. Moreover, they are more strongly aligned with refusal-target gradients than with correct helpful-target gradients on over-refusal prompts. This imbalanced gradient coupling can bias over-refusal samples toward refusal, while the reduced apparent representational separation in deeper layers may make it more difficult for the model to maintain a clear boundary between harmful and over-refusal samples. To this end, we propose GOAL (Gradient Orthogonalization with contrAstive Learning), a safety-alignment framework for mitigating over-refusal. Specifically, GOAL consists of two components: 1) to prevent imbalanced gradient coupling from biasing the optimization of over-refusal samples toward refusal, we orthogonalize over-refusal gradients against the low-rank refusal subspace extracted from harmful gradients; and 2) to explicitly enhance the separation between harmful and over-refusal representations, we introduce a contrastive learning objective. Extensive experiments across multiple models and benchmarks demonstrate that GOAL effectively mitigates over-refusal while preserving safety performance and general capabilities.
