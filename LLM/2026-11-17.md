## 1. Right Reason, Wrong Advice: Evaluating Recourse in LLM Explanations of Denied Requests
- **Authors**: Arunkumar V
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21545](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21545)
- **Abstract**:
> Language models increasingly write the explanations people receive when a rule system denies their request. If several rules deny the request independently, an explanation can name a true reason and still leave the person denied after they fix it. We call the missing property recourse validity: changing what the explanation cites, without changing what was asked for, can flip the decision. We compute it exactly, and a hitting-set argument predicts from the policy alone when a correct reason fails. In six of seven rule systems ported from access control and US benefit eligibility, a single correct reason almost never gives recourse when several reasons apply. Across 20 LLMs, checks of whether the cited reasons are correct, computed or asked of an LLM judge, are close to chance at spotting this failure, as the argument predicts. A strong judge asked whether acting on the explanation would work does spot it. Stronger and reasoning models do better but still fail on many denials with four or more independent reasons. Asking for every rule helps every open model with room to improve. Explanations of eligibility decisions should be built from the decision function and judged by whether acting on them works.

## 2. Procedure without Judgment: What Small Language Models Learn under an Exact Verifier
- **Authors**: Arunkumar V , Manoranjan Gandhudi , Gangadharan G. R. , Arun Prakash , S. Senthilkumar
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21529)
- **Abstract**:
> When an authorization policy blocks an agent's tool call, the audit log should name the facts that forced the decision and nothing more. For an executable policy over an enumerable context space this minimal sufficient reason can be computed exactly, so an explanation is scored against ground truth rather than a proxy for it. We use that exactness to ask what a model can learn from a verifier that has no error in it. The explainer sees one request and one decision, never the policy, and has thirty turns in which it may call the policy on altered requests and ask whether a candidate set of facts is sufficient. Frontier models find the exact reason about half the time with the tools alone, and one sentence telling them to shrink the answer lifts Haiku 4.5 from 58% to 75%. A 4B model fine-tuned on 2000 traces of the deletion algorithm reaches 92% on the policy it was trained on. Teacher kind is what moves this: at matched data size the algorithm-taught student leads the frontier-taught one in all three training seeds, and the two arms never overlap. What the student learns is a fixed schedule of sufficiency checks, and the schedule does not travel. On three public policies it never saw, its answers stay sufficient (94%) and stop being minimal (48%), while Haiku under the same sentence is exact on all 47 episodes. Reinforcement learning from the exact reward leaves held-out accuracy where it was and inverts the strategy, from probing the policy to spending the entire budget on sufficiency checks. The oracle made the effect of training legible, but it did not make training better.

## 3. Generative AI and the Crisis of Higher Education Assessments: Examining 'AI-Proofing' Strategies for College-Level Assessments
- **Authors**: Bonam Mingole , Davis Yadav , Hui Min Lee , S. Shyam Sundar , Amulya Yadav
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21476)
- **Abstract**:
> Higher education is confronting an academic-integrity challenge unlike any in its history. Students can now outsource homework, take-home assignments, and online examinations to large language models (LLMs) that complete them in seconds. Existing research on evaluating LLM performance on academic assessments has focused largely on standardized examinations, or on questions from college-level courses drawn from a single discipline. Far less is known about how LLMs perform on college-level assessment questions spanning multiple disciplines, that instructors have purposely designed to be AI-resistant, and about which features of such questions, if any, meaningfully constrain LLM performance. We address this gap by collecting 75 questions from authentic college coursework assessments, spanning 17 disciplines, which were contributed by 37 faculty members from ten U.S. universities and one U.K. university. Of these, 61 questions were intentionally designed by faculty members to be 'LLM-proof', while the remaining 14 questions were sourced from online courses. We then (i) evaluate three state-of-the-art LLMs' ability to correctly answer these questions with and without supplementary course materials; (ii) identify five strategies, through thematic analysis, that faculty used to make their questions harder for LLMs to solve; and (iii) test whether these strategies impact LLMs' ability to answer the questions accurately. Our experimental results show that the best-performing model scored approximately 83% without supplementary course materials and 85% with them, and we observed noteworthy differences across models, answer formats, and instructor tweaking strategies. We discuss implications for assessment design, academic-integrity policy, and the broader ethics of deploying LLMs in educational settings.

## 4. Learning to Reason with Predictive Gains for Generative Recommendation
- **Authors**: Zhuoyang Li , Teng Shi , Jiakai Tang , Weijie Yu , Guofu Xie , Xiao Zhang , Lei Kou , Yang Yu , Lei Shen , Xin Chen , Bing Wang , Xiaoyi Zeng , Jun Xu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21441)
- **Abstract**:
> Reasoning-enhanced generative recommendation (GR) has attracted increasing attention for leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to improve next-item prediction. However, existing methods primarily optimize reasoning through Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rule-based rewards determined by final recommendation performance, which faces two key limitations: (i) unmeasured predictive contributions within reasoning, where the predictive contributions of individual reasoning steps are not directly reflected in optimization signals, and (ii) weakly discriminative learning signals within groups, where different reasoning trajectories within the group often receive indistinguishable rewards, limiting effective policy optimization. In this paper, we propose GainReason, a simple yet effective training framework that optimizes reasoning through Predictive Gain (PG). Specifically, PG measures changes in target Semantic ID (SID) log-likelihood induced by individual reasoning sentences, providing contribution-aware and discriminative rewards. We further theoretically establish the alignment between expected PG and the recommendation ranking objective. Building on this signal, we develop PG-based Reasoning Optimization (PGRO) to optimize reasoning generation with sentence-level rewards, supported by a custom causal attention mechanism for efficient PG computation. Additionally, an auxiliary PG-based Reasoning Utilization (PGRU) task adaptively strengthens reasoning-conditioned prediction to complement PGRO. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks demonstrate that GainReason consistently outperforms competitive baselines and achieves substantial performance gains from reasoning.

## 5. Turing Agent: Agentic Formal Verification for Mathematical Research
- **Authors**: Yang Chen , Qianyi Wang , Ronghui Yang , Zhanhao Li , Yu Cong , Chen Zhang , Hangfan Zhang , Shao Zhang , Shuai Li , Toru Takisaka , Chao Xu , Yi Zhou , Zhihao Tang , Xiao Wang , Bo Zhang , Shuyue Hu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21299](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21299)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly advancing mathematical discovery, moving beyond benchmark-level problem solving to tackle frontier research problems. This progress is ushering mathematics into an era of proof abundance, raising a fundamental challenge: how can we ensure the correctness of the ever-growing volume of AI-generated proofs and transform them into trustworthy, machine-verified mathematical knowledge? We introduce Turing Agent, an agent harness designed to orchestrate end-to-end formal verification of research-level mathematics. Its wo-scale architecture integrates theorem formalization and proof formalization agents, empowered by structured proof decomposition, parallel verification, accumulated proof experience, and selective semantic review. Leveraging our collaborations with theoretical computer science (TCS) researchers, we focus our evaluation on TCS, a particularly demanding domain characterized by intricate quantification, complex dependencies, and stringent semantic requirements. Against direct generation and general-purpose coding harnesses, including Codex CLI and Claude Code, Turing Agent achieves approximately 57-62% faithful formalization success on Formal TCS Bench and 83% overall pass@1 on a TCS-focused subset of RLMEval, using GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro. Notably, Turing Agent, built on DeepSeek, successfully completes the formal verification of three previously unresolved TCS tasks from OpenAI's October 2026 collection. One particularly challenging TCS problem required about a hundred formally verified auxiliary claims. Moreover, by actively exploring and refining the formalization process, Turing Agent can uncover simpler alternative proofs, expose unnecessary dependencies in original proofs, and generate reusable mathematical knowledge as a byproduct of verification.

## 6. How Developer Personality Shapes the Use of AI Coding Assistants
- **Authors**: Annemarie Wittig , Alina Mailach , Norbert Siegmund
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21240](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21240)
- **Abstract**:
> Developers differ in personality traits that shape how they approach software development tasks, yet AI coding assistants either behave uniformly or offer personalization features whose fit with individual developers must be found through trial and error. This is because it remains unclear how personality shapes developers' experiences with AI assistants, and thus which traits personalization should account for. In other words, we provide tools with no underlying theory on what works when and why. We take a first step towards such a theory with a systematic scoping review on how users' personality relates to their perception of LLM-based tools. We identified twelve studies through general search engines, major Software Engineering (SE) venues, AI tooling, and snowballing. They all reported associations between personality and perceptions, particularly perceived usefulness, yet none addressed this in a SE context. Building on these findings, we conducted an exploratory two-wave survey with 29 and 11 developers, collecting first empirical evidence of how developers' personality (measured using the Big Five Model) relates to the perception of AI coding assistants. Higher Extraversion was associated with more positive private-use experiences in both waves, while Agreeableness was associated with negative professional experiences in the first wave and positive ones in the second. These results provide first indications that the personality effects found in the literature extend to developers. Although further studies with bigger samples are needed to build a theory, our work takes the first step towards an empirical grounded theory of developer personality and AI personalization traits.

## 9. Conflict-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation in Aligned Action Spaces for Cross-Game Generalization
- **Authors**: Jing Yi , Cehao Yang , Hualei Zhang , Haitao Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21094](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21094)
- **Abstract**:
> Zero-shot cross-game generalization aims to consolidate accessible source-game experts into a single vision-language model (VLM) that controls unseen games from raw pixels without target-game parameter updates. While multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) mitigates offline exposure bias and single-game reinforcement learning (RL) overfitting, scaling it to autoregressive VLMs faces two bottlenecks. First, token-to-action misalignment from subword length bias distorts discrete action probabilities and uncertainty margins. Second, multi-teacher policy conflict arises as source teachers under incompatible dynamics issue contradictory closed-loop corrections, causing negative transfer. We propose CAMOPD (Conflict-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation), a post-training framework that resolves multi-teacher policy conflict within an aligned semantic action space. CAMOPD first uses complete-action alignment to normalize command completion likelihoods over each game's legal action set, forming a calibrated categorical policy that provides reliable uncertainty margins for selective on-policy retention and visual specialist probes that can be evaluated across source games. To resolve policy conflict without target-game data, conflict-aware multi-teacher consolidation cross-evaluates these specialists across source games to capture both visual identifiability and inter-teacher compatibility, upweighting compatible teachers while suppressing conflicting corrections. On GameWorld's temporal and directional transfer suites with Qwen3.5-4B, CAMOPD achieves 26/30 (86.7%) and 24/30 (80.0%) Task-1 zero-shot success on unseen GeoDash and Doodle Jump, outperforming prompting, weight-merging, and multi-task baselines.

## 12. GRACE: Parallel Best-of-N Multi-Agent Analysis of Closed-Form Questions over Dataframes with Guardrail Selection and Adaptive Retry
- **Authors**: Fabio Picariello , Salvatore Cipolla , Susanna Bonura , Vito Morreale
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20983](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20983)
- **Abstract**:
> LLM agents answer closed-form questions over a tabular dataframe by writing and executing Python, almost always as a single rollout: one interpretation, one script, one result, accepted without comparison. The few pipelines that produce several candidates reconcile them by answer-agreement voting, which needs a single comparable scalar and always returns one, even when every candidate is wrong. We present GRACE (Generative, Ranked, Adaptive Code Ensemble), a production-oriented pipeline that instead runs parallel best-of-N selection specialised to dataframe code. N agents run concurrently under a strict, machine-checkable output contract, diversified by persona hints, per-retry rotation, and a two-regime temperature schedule. Rather than vote, GRACE judges the executed artefacts: a pairwise tournament pre-filters the pool and a domain-specialised guardrail either selects the best candidate or, when none is defensible, rejects the whole pool and abstains with a diagnosis (an option voting structurally lacks), which in turn drives an adaptive retry with per-agent feedback. On InfiAgent-DABench (DAEval, M = 246), using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B as the backbone, GRACE raises answer match from 39% for a single-rollout baseline to 71% (+32 pp, 1.8x), recovering 53% of previously wrong answers. On the same DAEval task, the smaller dense Qwen3.5-9B lifts answer match from 38% to 63% (+25 pp, 1.7x). A controlled 3 x 3 study that sweeps the number of parallel agents against the retry budget finds, on the 35B backbone, the two levers largely substitutable: once either one is generous, the other adds little, identifying a cheap operating point for deployment. The same directional pattern replicates on the independent DataBench benchmark, again on the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B backbone, where answer match rises by 15 percentage points (from 70% to 85%) across 400 sampled questions.

## 15. Multi-LLM Agentic AI at the Edge: Joint Agent Scheduling and Resource Allocation
- **Authors**: Yuanyan Song , Kezhi Wang , Xinmian Xu , Yongmin Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20915)
- **Abstract**:
> In this paper, an edge-enabled multi-large language model (LLM) agentic AI architecture is proposed, where one supervisor AI agent and multiple worker AI agents cooperatively execute users' requests at the edge. The supervisor AI agent decouples the user request into a series of sub-tasks and dynamically dispatches them to LLM-equipped worker AI agents via two agentic workflows, i.e., parallel and serial workflows, with a two-level retry mechanism to enhance execution reliability. ## 16. Conversing with a Conversational Agent: A Real Conversation, Not a Simulated One
- **Authors**: Christophe Denis (UMMISCO, IHPST)
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20826](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20826)
- **Abstract**:
> The claim that interactions with artificial conversational agents merely simulate human conversation rests on a philosophical distinction between reproducing the observable features of an activity and actually performing it. Although this distinction is appropriate for certain physical phenomena, its application to linguistic and symbolic activities is far less straightforward. This article argues that conversations with large language model (LLM)-based agents are genuine conversational activities rather than mere simulations, without thereby attributing consciousness, subjective understanding, or human intentionality to artificial systems. Drawing on the distinction between subjective and objective simulation, the article first examines the limitations of defining conversation exclusively through the intrinsic properties of its participants. It then develops a relational and semiotic account of conversational activity, informed by linguistic pragmatics, Lacan's Conversing with a Conversational Agent theory of the mirror, Baudrillard's concept of the simulacrum, Simondon's philosophy of individuation, and Peirce's semiotics. These perspectives make it possible to distinguish the reality of conversational interaction from the appearance of artificial subjectivity and from the epistemic validity of the meanings produced. The argument culminates in the concept of Hybrid Semiotic Fabrication, which characterizes meaning-making as a distributed process involving human participants, artificial intelligence, corpora, and technical arrangements. Recognizing the reality of human-AI conversation therefore entails neither anthropomorphizing artificial agents nor granting their outputs epistemic authority. Rather, it requires a more precise account of the relational, semiotic, and normative conditions under which conversational meaning is produced and justified.

## 17. ForeSay: Tell the Future in Words, Branch the Details in Noise for Precipitation Nowcasting
- **Authors**: Xudong Ling , Wenjie Luo , Chaorong Li , Tianxi Huang , Tao He , Guiduo Duan
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20815](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20815)
- **Abstract**:
> Precipitation nowcasting involves two distinct scales of uncertainty: macroscopic storm evolution and fine-scale local convective variations. Current generative ensemble methods entangle both scales within independent random noise draws. This coupling causes two practical limitations: every ensemble member must execute the full sampling trajectory from scratch, so an N-member ensemble costs N times a single forecast; moreover, divergent trajectories spatially misalign heavy-rain echoes across members, causing standard pixel-wise averaging to blur critical high-intensity extremes. We present ForeSay, a generative nowcasting framework that decouples macroscopic evolution prediction from local stochastic detail generation. Given historical radar frames, a vision-language model first predicts a textual scenario of future storm dynamics to condition a Rectified Flow generator. Guided by this shared macroscopic prior, ensemble members share early trunk sampling steps to establish the storm trajectory and branch with fresh noise only in later steps to explore local convective details. Because members then differ mainly in local details, self-anchored support-consensus decoding replaces pixel averaging with discrete support voting to preserve sharp heavy-rain cores. Across six datasets, ForeSay achieves the best CSI at the highest precipitation thresholds, with relative improvements over the strongest baseline on each dataset ranging from over 10% to 84%. Our 8-member ensemble retains the forecasting skill and diversity of eight independently sampled runs while reducing computational cost from 8x to 5.2x that of a single forecast.

## 18. Regularizing Treatment Leakage for LLM-based Uplift Estimation
