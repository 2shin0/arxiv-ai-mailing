## 1. Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting
- **Authors**: Shangyin Tan , Ben Zhou , Lakshya A Agrawal , Jialin Zhang , Yilin Chen , Qiuyang Mang , Donghyun Lee , Dennis Jacob , Koushik Sen , Alex Dimakis , Matei Zaharia
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20563)
- **Abstract**:
> Automated research with LLM agents has shown promising results in various domains. Through iterative refinement and scored verifiers, LLM agents are capable of discovering new ideas. There are different ways to scale automated research: let one agent iterate for longer, run several agents in parallel, or further let the parallel agents communicate and build on one another's candidates. However, we find that all three strategies eventually plateau as the agents accumulate history. Each agent becomes anchored to its own past attempts, making fewer and smaller edits. Beyond this point, additional budget brings little further improvement. In this paper, we show that the key to scaling discovery further is to forget. Periodically discarding the search state, including every agent's session and the candidate history, and restarting from the best candidate found so far, unblocks the search and pushes the same budget further. We implement forgetting in a team of agents with communication and evaluate it on four tasks spanning heuristic programming, kernel optimization and model training. The results show up to 15% improvement compared to parallel agents, and up to 39% compared to a single agent. Our results suggest that, for open-ended discovery, what an agent is allowed to remember matters as much as how much compute it is given.

## 2. PhySGA: A Physics-Space Grounded Agentic Framework for Generalizable and Interactive 3D Composition
- **Authors**: Weichen Lyu , Chong Xia , Mingyu Dong , Long Xu , Shengjun Zhang , Yueqi Duan
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20560](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20560)
- **Abstract**:
> 3D object composition is pivotal to digital content creation and robot simulation, while also serving as a critical testbed for physical intelligence in foundation models. Previous methods either leverage large language models to directly predict object coordinates, or utilize generative diffusion priors to synthesize composite images for visual alignment, both of which rely on static priors in digital spaces. However, these methods consistently struggle with complex compositions such as precise hanging, gravity-driven settling and hidden containment, as these tasks require interactive construction processes involving global planning, fine-grained alignment, and physical simulation. To address these challenges, we propose PhySGA, a Physics-Space Grounded Agentic Framework for 3D composition via agent exploration and physical simulation, enabling the universal interactive assembly of rigid, articulated, and deformable objects. Directly applying vanilla LLMs or VLMs to this task proves insufficient, as current models inherently lack grounded physical understanding and interactive construction experience. To overcome this limitation, we design a collaborative feedforward-feedback architecture: an Assembly Agent focuses on geometric and kinematic reasoning for global step planning and coarse adjustment, while a Critic Agent reasons over contact dynamics for simulator state diagnosis and fine-grained closed-loop correction. Furthermore, Action Routers map semantic planning and analysis onto executable physical primitives within the simulator, while a task-specific Self-Evolving Skill Library distills historical trial-and-error feedback into reusable experiences, boosting success rates and minimizing exploration attempts. Extensive experiments within Isaac Sim demonstrate that PhySGA significantly surpasses existing methods in relation fidelity, physical plausibility, and visual quality.

## 3. From Relevance to Validity: Zero-Shot Tool Selection via Functional and Contextual Validation
- **Authors**: Xiaoxuan Dong , Jin Zhang , Zichen Yuan , Yue Zhou , Zihao Rao , Dexu Yu , Tingyong WU , Wenhao Deng , Junchen Fu , Pengwei Sun , Yang Ran , Bo Lan , Yongxin Ni , Youhua Li
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20388)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly face tasks from unseen domains and execution contexts, making zero-shot tool selection essential for identifying tools that can satisfy task requirements. Existing dominant methods mainly rely on relevance ranking, but semantic matching alone cannot ensure that a tool is functionally compatible or remains applicable in a new context. We preliminarily identify a \emph{transfer-validity gap}, where semantically relevant tools may still fail to satisfy functional or contextual requirements after transfer. To address this gap, we propose \methodname{}, a progressive validation framework that complements semantic relevance with endpoint-grounded functional compatibility and task-conditioned contextual evidence. Across cross-domain and cross-dataset transfer, \methodname{} consistently improves capability ranking, with larger gains under functional or contextual ambiguity. Ablation results further confirm the complementary contributions of functional and contextual validation.

## 4. TreeFlex: Agents Navigating Their Own Search Trees
- **Authors**: Dongzhuo Li , Fangzhou Cheng , Weiwei Li , Kevin Han , Haixu Ma , Zhongchang Sun , Ruiyi Li , Serena Li , Xiangjun Fan , Lizhu Zhang , Yuandong Tian
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20334)
- **Abstract**:
> A ReAct-style agent accumulates observations, intermediate results, and failed attempts in a single context. As this context grows, stale and off-path information consumes its limited capacity and obscures relevant evidence, making focused reasoning increasingly difficult. We introduce TreeFlex, a framework in which the agent's own policy decides where to search. The agent records each intermediate state, with a short summary, as a node in a search tree maintained by the harness. The policy is a single language model that acts in two roles. In the Reflection role, it has a global view of the tree through these summaries and picks a node to resume, together with an instruction. In the Execution role, the harness restores the path that led to that node, and the model carries out the instruction with the usual task tools until it saves a new node or proposes a solution, which Reflection then approves or sends back to another node. Through these choices, the policy decides whether to deepen the current line, branch from or revisit an earlier state, verify a proposal, or stop, without a fixed traversal order or a score for each node. We train navigation, task actions, checkpointing, and proposal review jointly with a tree-aware adaptation of GRPO, using task-level outcome rewards augmented by process rewards. After small-scale RL of a 4B model, with the same active-context limit for all methods, TreeFlex reaches 48.8% accuracy on a challenging deep-research benchmark, versus 41.5% for flat ReAct and 42.8% for FoldAgent, and its Pass@8 is about ten points higher than either. On SWE-bench Verified, with all three systems trained with RL from the same 9B model, TreeFlex resolves 54.9% of tasks, versus 43.3% for FoldAgent and 45.2% for its step-wise variant.

## 5. Argument Graph Uncertainty: Quantifying Uncertainty from the Logical Structure of Reasoning Chains
- **Authors**: Simon Baur , Sumire Honda , Wojciech Samek , Jackie Ma
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20328)
- **Abstract**:
> Reliable uncertainty estimation is essential for deploying large language models in high-stakes reasoning tasks, yet most existing approaches rely on expensive sampling strategies or shallow token-level signals that ignore the internal logical structure of model outputs. We introduce Argument Graph Uncertainty, a post-hoc framework for multiple choice question tasks, that estimates model confidence directly from the logical structure of a single chain-of-thought reasoning trace. Our method uses either a lightweight instruction-tuned model or a simple deterministic rule to segment the reasoning chain into propositional units, then applies a NLI model to infer relational edges and construct two complementary argument graphs over these segments. The resulting edge scores are aggregated into a Dirichlet posterior over answer options, whose concentration yields calibrated uncertainty estimates reflecting both local step-to-step coherence and global logical consistency across the reasoning trace. Evaluated on GPQA, MMLU-Pro and TruthfulQA, our method consistently surpasses token-based and consistency-based uncertainty baselines across multiple reasoning models. We further demonstrate through extensive combination analyses that argument graph uncertainty captures an orthogonal signal to existing approaches, making it a complementary component in uncertainty ensembles. Crucially, our framework requires no additional calls to the evaluated model and relies entirely on small, efficient auxiliary models, producing fully interpretable, per-node uncertainty attributions at low computational cost.

## 6. Reflect After Failure: Brain-Inspired Self-Evolving Agents for Embodied Spatial Intelligence
- **Authors**: Xin Li , Xiaohuan Gu , Yifan Wang , Shaobin Ling , Tianli Shi , Jingao Xu , Zhimeng Yin , Dapeng Wu , Yun Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20312)
- **Abstract**:
> Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have made substantial progress in spatial reasoning, and self-evolving agents further extend these capabilities to embodied environments by learning from interactions. However, current self-evolution remains particularly challenging in embodied spatial tasks, complex and multi-step decisions from partial visual evidence making it difficult to determine why an execution fails. The failure may stem from misperception, insufficient exploration, or incorrect planning, yet execution feedback alone rarely reveals which component is responsible. As a result, simply accumulating interaction experience can reinforce ineffective strategies. To address this, we propose HiMeco, a brain-inspired self-evolving agent for embodied spatial intelligence, inspired by metacognitive monitoring and hippocampal memory consolidation. Through Hippocampal-Metacognitive Reflection, HiMeco diagnoses failures in perception-exploration-planning (PEP), then compares execution attempts to derive reusable strategies and guidance for avoiding recurring errors. It also abstracts interaction programs into parameterized skills, using evidence contracts and execution-based validation to determine their retention and reuse. These strategies and skills guide subsequent interactions, with memory updated only between rounds and model parameters kept frozen. HiMeco achieves state-of-the-art performance on ESI-Bench, and it also improves accuracy on EmbSpatial-Bench by 3.94 percentage points without evolution.

## 7. CASI: Credit Assignment via Set Intervention for Agentic Retrieval
- **Authors**: Xinyi Cheng , Tianfu Zhang , Jiwen Li , Xuesen Zhang , Shihao Yu , Pengfei Zhu , Renhuan , Heda Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20293)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language models increasingly generate multiple retrieval actions jointly, while downstream systems often provide only one reward for the resulting set. Broadcasting this reward provides a valid score-function gradient estimator for the expected set-level objective, but does not determine a coalition-dependent within-set attribution. We introduce CASI (Credit Assignment via Set Intervention for Agentic Retrieval), which constructs the missing counterfactual information through snapshot-grounded set intervention: generate and retrieve once, hold replayable snapshot components fixed, and evaluate alternative subsets under common conditions. Shapley aggregation converts these marginals into within-set credit, which bounded zero-sum advantage shaping incorporates into policy optimization. For m >= 4, singleton, leave-one-out, and full-set endpoints do not generally determine the Shapley allocation; under additive set values, extra coalition evaluations are redundant. In a five-action sponsored-search setting, exhaustive auction replay over 1,200 requests reveals substantial coalition dependence. Under the training-time evaluator, CASI achieves the highest median cosine similarity to a replay-defined Shapley reference among evaluated credit rules; under a sensitivity backend, CASI does not outperform Singleton. Across five independent training runs, CASI-trained policies achieve higher held-out auction-replay set value than Singleton- or Broadcast-trained policies. In a randomized seven-day production A/B test on 10% of traffic, the CASI-trained policy yields an observed 3.51% lift in the primary advertiser-value metric over production control.

## 8. Drivers of LLM Consensus Formation Under Competing Incentives: A Simulation Study
- **Authors**: Isabel Jones , Matthew Edwards , Janina A. Hoffmann
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20200)
- **Abstract**:
> Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent environments that produce unanimous agreement, both in intended interactions that need coordination and in unintended emergent interactions. Consequently, understanding how LLMs with competing incentives reach consensus is crucial to better interpret and design LLM collectives. We introduce an evaluation, based on the Network Consensus game, to assess the mechanisms driving this process. Here, a network of agents must agree on one of two colours to receive a payoff, but agents are offered different payoffs for the agreed colour. We vary components such as communication access and network structure and assess six open-source models and one closed model (ChatGPT 5.6-sol). We find that open-source LLMs reach consensus less often than humans in comparable experiments and only if messages are communicated across the global network. ChatGPT 5.6-sol, however, is able to reach consensus with communication limited to its neighbours and with no communication ability. This is achieved through lower stubbornness and higher majority-following tendencies, and by providing more information about its neighbours' states in messages. However, situating agents in a highly clustered graph reduces consensus, regardless of these traits, demonstrating that both system design and individual model traits drive collective outcomes.

## 9. HEM-Med: A Hierarchical Agentic Memory Framework for Safe Multi-Center Medication Recommendation
- **Authors**: Min Xiao , Jin Li , Shoujin Wang , Yeguang Zhen , Yishuo Li , Wenpeng Lu

- **Abstract**:

While existing MR methods are mostly designed for single-center learning, where only one independent hospital is involved, multi-center MR with hospitals of different scales has become increasingly demanding in practice. In multi center settings, abundant data from large medical centers can improve recommendation performance for low-resource centers. Despite a few preliminary attempts on this task, two critical gaps remain. To address Gap 2, we introduce a safety-aware medication set generation module, which integrates dual-level experience retrieval with safety-aware set decoding to produce clinically relevant, safer, and locally adaptive medication recommendations. Experiments on a real-world multi-center eICU dataset show that HEM-Med outperforms representative transfer learning, LLM-based, and MR baselines in terms of both recommendation accuracy and safety.

## 10. LLM-Based Policy Compliance Verification of Loan Applications
- **Authors**: Florentina Voboril
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20155)
- **Abstract**:
> Banks rely on extensive internal policy frameworks to ensure that corporate lending decisions comply with regulatory requirements and internal risk standards. Reviewing loan applications against these policies is a largely manual and knowledge-intensive process, which can lead to overlooked policy violations or exceptions. This report presents a prototype of an LLM-based policy checker that automatically verifies loan applications against internal lending policies. The proposed approach follows a two-step architecture. First, compliance-relevant rules are automatically extracted from policy documents and converted into structured, machine-readable representations. Second, loan applications are evaluated against the extracted rules to identify policy exceptions, non-compliant findings, missing information, and cases requiring manual review. ## 11. EvalBuilder: Can LLMs Build Efficient Benchmarks?
- **Authors**: Huaisheng Zhu , Hangfan Zhang , Teng Xiao , Siyuan Xu , Shijie Zhou , Zhimeng Guo , Zhengyu Chen , Jingang Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20099)
- **Abstract**:
> Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly used for long-horizon research and engineering, yet evaluating these capabilities remains expensive, time-consuming, and heavily dependent on human expertise. This motivates a natural question: can LLM agents themselves construct efficient benchmarks that preserve the conclusions of costly reference evaluations? We study this capability by formulating automatic benchmark development as a meta-evaluation problem, where an agent is given a task and an expensive reference benchmark and requested to construct a lower-cost proxy benchmark that faithfully distinguishes among candidate models and solutions. Based on this formulation, we introduce EvalBuilder, a framework for evaluating such benchmark-generation capabilities along three key dimensions: fidelity, efficiency, and robustness. We further provide an iterative environment in which agents can refine their benchmarks under a fixed time budget. Our framework provides a systematic testbed and benchmark for understanding when current LLM agents can develop useful evaluators and how benchmark construction itself can be increasingly automated.

## 13. Beyond Correctness: Compute-Responsive Data Selection for Reasoning Model Post-Training
- **Authors**: Liang He , Xiangyu Li , Yuxin Wang , Hanbin Zhang , Mingyu Cao , Zhaomiao Guo , Xilu Wang
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20057)
- **Abstract**:
> Reasoning language models increasingly rely on test-time compute scaling, yet most existing work studies how to allocate inference computation while treating the model itself as fixed. We ask a complementary question: can reasoning models be post-trained to make better use of a given amount of test-time compute, and which examples are most useful for this goal? Our key observation is that reasoning problems differ substantially in their response to additional computation. Some are already solved under limited compute, others remain unresolved even after further reasoning, while a distinct subset changes from complete initial failure to success when additional reasoning is provided. We call these compute-responsive (CR) examples and introduce Compute-Responsive Data Selection (CRDS), a post-training data-selection principle that prioritizes examples with empirically demonstrated marginal utility from additional reasoning. On Qwen3-8B, SFT on CR examples recovers 45.3\% of held-out low-compute failures, compared with 40.6\% when training on already-solved examples. The advantage extends beyond the selected CR split: SFT-CR reaches 51.8\% overall accuracy on an independently sampled test distribution, with the gain concentrated on compute-responsive problems. CR training also shifts the compute--accuracy frontier across inference budgets from 512 to 4,096 tokens, outperforming matched non-CR training throughout and reaching 48.1\% recovery at the largest budget. The same pattern is supported across multiple data sources, remains visible under on-policy distillation, and extends to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct when CR sets are reconstructed separately for that model. Better test-time compute utilization can therefore be learned through post-training, with recoverable responses to additional reasoning providing a particularly informative signal for selecting training examples.

## 14. When Protection Overrides Consequences: Understanding Protection Mode in Large Language Model Decision-Making
- **Authors**: Yuanfan Li , Junyi Chen , Qi Zhou , Rui Yang , ZhangLiujie , Weihang Chen
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19948)
- **Abstract**:
> Frontier language models excel at complex tasks yet may prioritize protection over larger physical consequences in simple, urgent decisions. GPT-5.6 Sol, for example, moves a girl playing in shallow water before retrieving a submerged camera. We call this Protection Mode and study it with ProtectionBench: 285 prompts, 34 physical families, and nine models. At lowest effort, at least one of four responses prioritizes the subject on 34.74% of Sol prompts and over 83% of MiniMax M3 and Qwen3.5-27B prompts; none of four independent human raters does so. Controls show that choices usually track physical risk. We elicit both risks before requesting an action. Some models assess risk incorrectly; others state the correct ordering but still choose the subject. Qwen3.5-27B with thinking shows 100% correct risk ordering but 57.81% subject choice among eligible conversations on eight selected scenes. Attention audits find no stable reduction in attention to relevant facts; removing the stated risk direction reduces object-first choice in dots3-note-prev but not detectably in Qwen3.5-27B. Adding a general "people or animals before property" rule increases subject choice by 19-38 percentage points, whereas factual reminders favor the object by 0.7-5.2 points. Removing naturally generated versions of that rule lowers subject choice by 10-15 points. We also examine whether post-training introduces this pattern. Base models already show Protection Mode; post-training changes justifications more than choices.

## 15. Sparse Transformer: a Controlled Testbed for Mechanistic Interpretability
- **Authors**: Anton Korznikov , Alexey Dontsov , Elena Tutubalina
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19907](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19907)
- **Abstract**:
> Mechanistic interpretability aims to decompose neural network computations into human-understandable features, yet progress is limited by the absence of ground-truth units to evaluate against. We address this with a Sparse Transformer (ST): a 5-layer language model trained on TinyStories whose wide, TopK-sparse MLP forces neuron specialization. We show that ST neurons are highly interpretable: they outperform a loss-matched dense transformer by 43% on AutoInterp, and circuits of a median of 12 neurons suffice to reproduce individual model predictions. This testbed allows us to directly compare feature learning methods: Transcoder, which approximates the MLP input-output mapping, recovers ST's neuron vectors with 52.6% higher cosine similarity than Sparse Autoencoder, which only reconstructs outputs. Furthermore, the ST enables a controlled study of polysemanticity: distilling its sparse MLP into a dense counterpart reveals how compression leads to feature superposition. These results show how a known reference basis sharpens evaluation and understanding, a role we hope the ST can serve for the field.

- **Authors**: Tianyu Liu , Davide Belli , Bence Major , Arash Behboodi , Tribhuvanesh Orekondy

## 17. One Monitor Does Not Fit All: Formalism-Agnostic Runtime Verification for Agentic AI
- **Authors**: Angelo Ferrando
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19818](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19818)
- **Abstract**:
> Agentic AI systems combine model decisions, mutable state, tool calls, and delegation to external agents. Monitoring them requires observations from different execution boundaries, while prevention requires control before an operation produces its effect. We present AgenticRV, a runtime-verification architecture that separates these boundaries from the monitoring formalism. Adapters emit canonical events; each monitor receives its own projection and abstraction; and a decision policy uses normalised verdicts to select a reaction supported by the responsible adapter. The results support reuse of monitoring methods at explicit control boundaries, subject to the stated observation and deployment assumptions.

## 20. Articulation Upper Bounds for Language Models
- **Authors**: Alexander Spangher , Daniel E. Ho , Diyi Yang , Noah Goodman , Sanmi Koyejo
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19721](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19721)
- **Abstract**:
> Language captures only a subset of the perceptions, intuitions and intents that form meaning between speakers. What, then, are the limits of language? We take steps towards measuring this in LLMs as follows: for a set of concepts, we measure how well each concept can be transmitted between two LLMs as their capabilities scale. This requires novel measurement tools: (1) optimal messages: we introduce a prompt optimization algorithm that beats existing algorithms by up to 14% on benchmarks and accurately estimates prompt upper bounds. (2) A transmission measure: the sender is asked to label text; the receiver then tries to recover the sender's prompt, and recovery is measured; we find this correlates strongly with human label error. With these measures, we use observational scaling laws to study how transmissibility of a concept, conveyed with optimal messages, increases as transmitter and receiver capacity grows: does it asymptote below or above a threshold? Measuring 1.2k concepts in eight domains, we find concepts that (a) can be fully articulated; these concepts usually depend on local patterns, e.g. "joke ends with surprise"; (b) might be articulated by future, stronger models; these rely on whole-text structural patterns, e.g. "story's worldbuilding coherence"; (c) concepts that face articulability limits; these concepts' meaning, we find, is tied to identity and social-group awareness, e.g. "comic voice". Interesting, these same concepts are not all challenging for humans: humans have moderate-to-high levels of agreement in 76% of concepts that LLMs struggle with; articulation limits, we find, are executor relative. Finally, we characterize the nature of improvement in each case: while recoverable concepts can be articulated through definitions and explanations, irrecoverable concepts cannot be transmitted, even with examples, and might require novel learning mechanisms.

## 21. RuleTrek: Benchmarking Agentic Rule Reasoning in Complex Scenarios
- **Authors**: Chenhao Yuan , Bohan Yu , Yinhao Xu , Chao Yin , Chenxi Zhou , Zhaoxuan Wang , Shuwen Xu , Shaoping Huang , Haolin Ren , Jiaxiang Liu , Yao Xu , Jun Zhao , Kang Liu
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19698)
- **Abstract**:

## 134. Lotus: Learning to Understand Time-series by Learning to Forecast
- **Authors**: Van Dai Do , Huu Hiep Nguyen , Minh Hoang Nguyen , Manh Nguyen , Hung Le
- **URL**: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18877)
